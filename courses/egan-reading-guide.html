Key characters: three fish.

Compare body shape first. These historical illustrations are not to the same scale.

No. 01Chapter 2

Lake trout

The native predator

Salvelinus namaycush

Spot it: Light spots. Dark body. Deeply forked tail.

A long-lived, cold-water hunter. Egan makes its collapse the measure of a food web coming apart.

Predator ≠ parasite. The trout eats other fish; the lamprey attaches to a host.

No. 02Chapter 2

Sea lamprey

The parasitic newcomer

Petromyzon marinus

Spot it: Long, round body. Sucker mouth. No paired fins.

Its canal-assisted spread into the upper lakes puts a new pressure on native fish. Larvae hidden in streambeds become the key to control.

An eel-like fish, but not an eel. “Invasive” here refers to sea lampreys in the upper Great Lakes.

No. 03Chapter 2

Alewife

The small fish with a big effect

Alosa pseudoharengus

Spot it: Silvery sides. Flattened body. Large eye.

A schooling, plankton-eating herring. As predators disappear, its population surges; enormous die-offs make ecological disruption impossible to ignore.

A different species, not a young lake trout. Its abundance creates the next management dilemma.

The humans: big plans, uneven consequences.

Jesse Hawley

The idea · ch. 1

A flour merchant in debtors’ prison imagines an inland canal. Watch an economic problem become a plan to remake geography.

DeWitt Clinton

The political force · ch. 1

New York’s governor champions the Erie Canal. Egan’s engineering triumph depends on political persistence as well as invention.

George Hickey

The cost · ch. 1, pp. 33–34

A resident of Moulinette whose home must move for the Seaway project. His memories turn “progress” into a question about homes, graves, and belonging.

Pat Kenney

The question · ch. 1, p. 35

An eighth grader who writes to President Eisenhower about the danger to freshwater fish. Notice who asks the question the experts miss.

Lewis G. Castle

The reassurance · ch. 1, p. 35

The Seaway official who answers Kenney. His explanation of outward-flowing freshwater overlooks the organisms ships can carry in ballast water.

No verified portraits are shown for Hickey, Kenney, or Castle.

Great Lakes Science Hall of Fame

Lab coats, please. Read for the work—and for the different visions of what a healthy lake should be.

Vernon Applegate

Understand it to control it

Tracks the lamprey’s life cycle and leads the search for a treatment that can kill lampreys while sparing other fish. His aim: make native lake trout recovery possible.

Howard Tanner

A different future?

Appears at the end of chapter 2 with a competing vision: manage the altered lakes by introducing a new predator. Hold that question for the next reading.

Science is a team sport. Louis King and Cliff Kortman belong in this story too: years of chemical testing, dangerous working conditions, and bottle after bottle. Kortman began as a janitor. See pp. 62–64.

One more memorable cameo: James J. Strang

The self-proclaimed king on Beaver Island also observes differences among lake trout. Egan uses this unlikely observer to introduce the diversity hidden inside one species. See chapter 2, pp. 42–44.

Major arguments: think in tensions.

Philosophical lenses for reading—not Egan’s own headings, and not necessarily either/or choices.

Progress vs. Precaution

Ethics of risk

Canals and the Seaway promise prosperity while making ecological surprises possible.

How much uncertainty should we accept before remaking a shared environment?

Human usefulness vs. Ecological integrity

Environmental ethics

A shipping corridor can be economically useful while weakening the lake’s living relationships.

Is a lake’s value exhausted by what it can do for people?

Collective prosperity vs. Local justice

Distributive justice

The Seaway’s regional benefits arrive with the loss of Hickey’s community.

Who gains, who pays, and who gets a say?

Expert authority vs. Critical inquiry

Epistemology: how we know

Castle offers a correct fact about water flow; Kenney’s larger concern remains unanswered.

When does confidence in one fact become blindness to another question?

Targeted control vs. Systems thinking

Causation and interdependence

Suppressing lampreys helps trout, but does not simply reset the whole food web. Alewives complicate the success story.

Can we solve one problem without changing the conditions for another?

Restoration vs. Redesign

The meaning of “nature”

Applegate hopes to recover native trout; Tanner points toward a newly managed fishery.

Does repair mean returning to an earlier world—or making a different one work?

Emotional moves: what does Egan make a reader feel?

Track the effect of the writing: its images, sequence, contrasts, and choice of people.

  1. WonderThe scale of the lakes and the audacity of cutting new waterways.
  2. UneaseThe story narrows from engineering achievement to Hickey’s displaced home.
  3. HorrorLamprey bodies and damaged fish give the ecological crisis a visceral form.
  4. SuspenseRepeated lab failures turn the search for a selective poison into a race.
  5. Relief → uncertaintyA treatment works. Then the alewife problem complicates the victory.
