Thought experiments & metaphors

Five thinkers. Images that carry an argument.

Compare what each image makes visible about human nature, learning, government, or knowledge. Most passage links open the current course packets; page references identify the source editions. Entries marked “Further example” go beyond the concentrated readings.

All examples · all thinkers

Major thought experiments, metaphors, and parables in Mengzi, Laozi, Zhuangzi, Xunzi, and Han Feizi, with passage references.
ThinkerImage or experimentWhat happensWhat it helps arguePassage
Mengzi Fifty paces laughing at a hundredAnalogy Soldiers who flee fifty paces mock those who flee a hundred; both have run away. A king whose government is only less harmful than his neighbors’ has not yet achieved benevolent rule. Mengzi 1A3Van Norden, p. 122
Mengzi Blaming the year or the weaponAnalogy A ruler blames famine deaths on the bad year, like a killer blaming the weapon. Rulers remain responsible for preventable suffering; pointing to external conditions does not excuse failed government. Mengzi 1A3Bloom, p. 4
Mengzi Leading animals to devour peopleMetaphor The ruler’s animals are well fed while people starve; Mengzi compares death through government with death by a blade. Lavish provision for the ruler alongside neglected human hunger makes government itself destructive. Mengzi 1A4; 3B9Bloom, p. 4; Van Norden, p. 138
Mengzi King, ox, and sheepDialogue A king spares a frightened ox but substitutes a sheep he has not seen. Compassion can be real yet selective; the king should extend it to his people. Mengzi 1A7Van Norden, pp. 123–124
Mengzi Lifting Mount Tai or helping an elderThought experiment Carrying Mount Tai over the sea is impossible; massaging an elder’s stiff joints is not. The king fails to use capacities he has, like strength not used to lift a feather. Failure to extend compassion is a failure to act, not proof that benevolent government is beyond the king’s ability. Mengzi 1A7Van Norden, p. 124
Mengzi Trapping the peopleMetaphor A ruler leaves people without a secure livelihood, then punishes the crimes into which they fall. Benevolent rule must make ordinary life sustainable; punishment alone ignores the conditions government creates. Mengzi 1A7Van Norden, p. 126
Mengzi The tyrant as a mere fellowDialogue Asked about killing a ruler, Mengzi describes the executed tyrant Zhou as someone who had already forfeited the standing of a ruler. A political title depends on fulfilling moral duties; tyranny can strip its holder of the protection that the title normally gives. Mengzi 1B8Van Norden, p. 128
Mengzi Pulling up seedlingsParable An impatient farmer pulls his seedlings upward to help them grow; they wither. Moral development needs patient cultivation: neither neglect growth nor force it. Mengzi 2A2Van Norden, p. 130
Mengzi Child at the wellThought experiment Someone suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well and feels alarm and concern before calculating personal advantage. People have a spontaneous beginning of compassion; the example does not guarantee that everyone will carry out a rescue. Mengzi 2A6Van Norden, pp. 132–133
Mengzi Four sproutsMetaphor Compassion, shame, deference, and moral judgment are young sprouts; developing them is like a fire catching or a spring breaking through. These beginnings of benevolence, righteousness, ritual propriety, and wisdom need cultivation to develop fully. Mengzi 2A6Van Norden, pp. 132–133
Mengzi Parents’ bodies abandoned in a ditchImagined origin story People see animals feeding on their unburied parents and visibly recoil, without trying to impress anyone. Spontaneous feeling for parents helps ground burial practices; Mengzi challenges the Mohist account of impartial care. Mengzi 3A5Van Norden, p. 136
Mengzi The wide house and great WayMetaphor The great person inhabits the world’s wide house and walks its great Way, remaining steadfast through wealth, poverty, and coercion. Moral integrity, not the ability to frighten rulers or obtain office, defines greatness. Mengzi 3B2Bloom, p. 62
Mengzi Drowning sister-in-law; drowning worldThought experiment One must grasp a drowning sister-in-law despite a rule against touching. Saving a disordered world, however, requires the Way rather than a literal helping hand. Moral judgment can require departing from a ritual rule; extending an analogy also requires attention to the different case. Mengzi 4A17Van Norden, pp. 140–141
Mengzi Heaven’s eyes and earsMetaphor Heaven does not announce a successor: Shun’s conduct, orderly administration, and the people’s acceptance reveal its judgment. Political legitimacy requires more than a ruler’s private gift of the realm; Heaven’s judgment is manifested through the people. Mengzi 5A5Bloom, pp. 103–104
Mengzi Willow tree made into cups and bowlsDisputed analogy Gaozi compares moral formation to carving willow wood; Mengzi objects that carving violates the tree. Mengzi rejects a model that makes goodness an injury imposed on human nature from outside. Mengzi 6A1Van Norden, p. 146
Mengzi Water flowing downwardMetaphor Water naturally flows downward, although an external force can drive it upward. Human nature tends toward goodness; circumstances can divert that tendency. Mengzi 6A2Van Norden, p. 146
Mengzi Ox MountainMetaphor Logging and grazing strip a once-wooded mountain until it appears naturally barren. Damaged moral capacities do not show that human nature was originally bad; nourishment and conditions matter. Mengzi 6A8Van Norden, pp. 150–151
Mengzi Fish and bear’s pawAnalogy When two desired foods cannot both be had, one chooses the preferred one; Mengzi compares this with choosing righteousness over life. People can rank desires: preserving life is not necessarily their highest commitment. Mengzi 6A10Van Norden, p. 151
Mengzi Food given with contemptThought experiment A hungry person may refuse food offered insultingly, yet someone accepts a lavish salary without checking whether it is right. Moral shame can outweigh survival; wealth can nevertheless lead people to abandon the judgment they once upheld. Mengzi 6A10Van Norden, pp. 151–152
Mengzi A child’s love for parentsExample Young children love their parents without studying; older children recognize respect for elder siblings. Mengzi finds beginnings of benevolence and righteousness in ordinary relationships and calls for extending them more widely. Mengzi 7A15Van Norden, p. 153
Laozi Filled bellies and strong bonesPolitical image Sage rulers fill bellies and strengthen bones while weakening ambitions and reducing knowledge and desires. Order depends on meeting basic needs and curbing competitive desires; the ruler actively shapes the people. Daodejing 3Ivanhoe, p. 174
Laozi WaterMetaphor Water nourishes everything, occupies low places, and overcomes what is hard despite its softness. Humility, non-contention, and flexibility can be more effective than force. Daodejing 8, 78Ivanhoe, p. 176; pp. 208–209
Laozi Empty hub, vessel, and roomMetaphor Wheels, pots, and rooms work because of their empty spaces. Absence and openness make useful activity possible; solidity alone is insufficient. Daodejing 11Ivanhoe, p. 177
Laozi The scarcely noticed rulerThought experiment Rulers range from barely noticed to loved, feared, and despised. Under the best, people regard their order as simply how things are. Effective leadership need not advertise itself or make people feel dependent on a ruler. Daodejing 17Ivanhoe, p. 180
LaoziFurther example Uncarved blockMetaphor Unworked wood represents simplicity before carving divides it into specialized objects. Preserve simplicity; avoid excessive desires and imposed distinctions. Daodejing 28, 32Ivanhoe, pp. 186, 188
Laozi The world as a spiritual vesselMetaphor The world is likened to a sacred vessel: those who try to manipulate or grasp it ruin or lose it. The world cannot safely be treated as an object for a ruler’s controlling purposes. Daodejing 29Ivanhoe, p. 186
Laozi Weeds after war; victory as mourningPolitical image An army leaves thorns and failed harvests; even victory calls for mourning. Military force creates lasting damage; it should be a last resort, and killing should never be celebrated. Daodejing 30–31Ivanhoe, pp. 186–187
Laozi The ritualist rolls up his sleevesVignette When others fail to respond, the person insisting on ritual correctness rolls up his sleeves and resorts to force. Enforced ritual can reveal depleted trust and produce coercion instead of harmony. Daodejing 38Ivanhoe, p. 190
Laozi The rich court and empty granariesPolitical image Courts glitter while fields grow weeds and granaries stand empty; rulers consume too much in taxes while the people go hungry. Elite consumption can impoverish the people; luxury amid deprivation resembles robbery, not good government. Daodejing 53, 75Ivanhoe, pp. 197, 207
LaoziFurther example InfantMetaphor A baby is physically soft yet holds firmly and embodies vitality and harmony. Genuine strength can coexist with softness and freedom from calculated striving. Daodejing 55Ivanhoe, p. 198
Laozi Cooking small fishMetaphor Governing a large state is compared to cooking delicate fish. A common reading: restrained government avoids damaging what it seeks to nourish through excessive handling. Daodejing 60Ivanhoe, p. 200
Laozi The sea below the valleysMetaphor Rivers and the sea receive the valleys’ waters because they occupy the lower position. Leaders gain authority by placing themselves below and behind the people, rather than competing with them. Daodejing 66Ivanhoe, p. 204
Laozi Soft bodies, stiff corpses, breaking treesMetaphor Living bodies are soft; corpses are rigid. An overly rigid tree breaks. Flexibility belongs to life; apparent hardness can signal fragility and decline. Daodejing 76Ivanhoe, pp. 207–208
Laozi Small communities that never visitThought experiment A small population leaves labor-saving tools unused; neighbors hear one another’s animals but never visit. An ideal of local contentment challenges expansion, technological ambition, and restless travel. Daodejing 80Ivanhoe, pp. 209–210
Zhuangzi Useless treeParable A nimble weasel dies in a trap; a crooked tree escapes the carpenter and offers a place to rest. Conventional usefulness can endanger life; apparent uselessness can protect freedom. Zhuangzi 1Kjellberg, p. 229
Zhuangzi Giant bird and small creaturesParable A giant fish, Kun (Minnow), becomes Peng (Breeze). Small flyers mock its journey; short-lived mushrooms and insects cannot know longer cycles. Limited experience can make other lives seem absurd; scale and lifespan shape what a creature can understand. Zhuangzi 1Kjellberg, pp. 224–225
Zhuangzi Giant gourdsParable Huizi smashes enormous gourds that fail as containers and ladles; Zhuangzi proposes using them as floats. Usefulness depends on imagination and context; familiar purposes can conceal new possibilities. Zhuangzi 1Kjellberg, pp. 228–229
Zhuangzi Butterfly dreamThought experiment Zhou dreams he is a butterfly; awake, he wonders whether he is a butterfly dreaming he is Zhou. Questions certainty about identity and perspective; evokes the transformation of things. Zhuangzi 2Kjellberg, p. 238
Zhuangzi Wind and the ten thousand pipesMetaphor One wind blows through differently shaped hollows, producing many distinct sounds. Different forms yield different voices; the image invites reflection on how perspectives arise without a single imposed voice. Zhuangzi 2Kjellberg, pp. 229–230
Zhuangzi Whose home, taste, or beauty?Thought experiment Humans, eels, and monkeys prefer different homes; animals eat different foods and flee women celebrated as beautiful. Human standards are not automatically universal; judgments arise from particular forms of life. Zhuangzi 2Kjellberg, p. 236
Zhuangzi Who can judge the dispute?Thought experiment A proposed judge already agrees or disagrees with the disputants. Choosing another person does not by itself settle who is right. Winning an argument or appointing a judge cannot simply be assumed to establish an independent standard of truth. Zhuangzi 2Kjellberg, p. 238
Zhuangzi Cook DingParable His knife follows the spaces within an ox’s joints, remaining sharp for years. At difficult joints, he slows down. Practiced responsiveness to a situation’s structure preserves both skill and life. Zhuangzi 3Kjellberg, pp. 239–240
Zhuangzi Hundun / All-full (Chaos)Parable Two rulers repay Hundun’s kindness by boring seven human openings into him; he dies. Imposing supposedly beneficial standards can destroy another being’s way of life. Zhuangzi 7Kjellberg, p. 256
Zhuangzi Heart-mind as a mirrorMetaphor The perfected person’s heart-mind responds like a mirror, reflecting what comes without holding on to it. Receptive, adaptable action avoids clinging to preconceptions and possessions; emptiness need not mean inactivity. Zhuangzi 7Kjellberg, p. 256
Zhuangzi Happiness of fishDialogue Huizi challenges Zhuangzi’s claim to know that swimming fish are happy. Explores how we know other beings’ experiences and the limits of argumentative certainty. Zhuangzi 17Kjellberg, pp. 260–261
Zhuangzi Turtle in the mudParable Offered office, Zhuangzi asks whether a turtle would prefer honored bones or life in the mud. Living freely can be worth more than prestigious political service. Zhuangzi 17Kjellberg, p. 260
Zhuangzi Frog in the collapsed wellParable A frog boasts about its well until a sea turtle, too large to enter, describes the ocean. A familiar world can feel complete until a wider perspective exposes its limits. Zhuangzi 17Kjellberg, pp. 259–260
Zhuangzi Singing after his wife’s deathParable After first grieving, Zhuangzi beats a tub and sings, understanding his wife’s death as another transformation like the seasons. Seeing death within continuing transformation can alter mourning; the story explicitly acknowledges his initial grief. Zhuangzi 18Kjellberg, p. 261
Zhuangzi Cicada, mantis, magpie, and hunterParable Zhuangzi pursues a magpie hunting a mantis stalking a cicada—then is chased by the grove’s warden. Fixation on an attractive prize can hide one’s own vulnerability; even Zhuangzi can become entangled. Zhuangzi 20Kjellberg, p. 263
Xunzi Shaping wood and sharpening metalMetaphor Crooked wood is straightened, a straight beam bent into a lasting wheel, and dull metal sharpened. Teaching, ritual, and deliberate practice can produce lasting changes in dispositions; improvement need not leave the original material unchanged. Xunzi 1, 23Hutton, pp. 270–271, 310
Xunzi Blue dye and iceMetaphor Dye exceeds its plant’s color; ice is colder than water. Learning can transform people beyond their original condition. Xunzi 1Hutton, p. 270
Xunzi Small steps and accumulating streamsMetaphor Earth accumulates into a mountain and water into a deep pool; long journeys and rivers depend on small steps and streams. Moral achievement comes through accumulated, sustained practice. Xunzi 1Hutton, pp. 271–272
Xunzi Same infant cries, different customsExample Children begin with the same cries but grow up with different customs through teaching. Education and social surroundings shape conduct; acquired differences should not simply be treated as inborn. Xunzi 1Hutton, pp. 270–271
Xunzi Chariot and horses; boat and oarsAnalogy A rider travels far without better feet; a passenger crosses water without becoming a swimmer. Learning includes using external resources and supports well; cultivated ability need not come from exceptional birth. Xunzi 1Hutton, p. 271
Xunzi Old nags and persistent carvingAnalogy A fine horse’s single leap is limited; a nag’s continued travel and carving that cuts metal and stone show what persistence achieves. Persistence and accumulated effort can achieve what a brief display of talent cannot. Xunzi 1Hutton, pp. 271–272
Xunzi Humans commanding oxen and horsesComparison Humans are weaker than oxen and slower than horses, yet put both to use. Cooperation organized through shared standards, roles, and divisions gives human communities their strength. Xunzi 9Hutton, p. 281
Xunzi Ruler as boat, people as waterMetaphor Water carries a boat but can also overturn it; Xunzi applies the received saying to rulers and common people. The ruler’s security depends on governing fairly and caring for the people whose support sustains the state. Xunzi 9Hutton, Complete Text, p. 70
Xunzi Falling stars and human ill omensComparison People fear falling stars and groaning trees, while neglected fields and disordered government bring famine. The dangers to address arise from human mismanagement, not a supposed moral message in unusual natural events. Xunzi 17Hutton, p. 285
Xunzi Rain after a rain sacrificeCausal test Rain falls after a sacrifice, but it also falls without one. Xunzi retains the ritual while rejecting the proposed causal explanation. Ritual gives human affairs proper form; its value need not depend on controlling the weather. Xunzi 17Hutton, p. 286
Xunzi Potter and clayMetaphor A potter deliberately makes a vessel from clay. Sages create ritual and moral standards through cultivated activity, rather than receiving them as spontaneous products of inborn nature. Xunzi 23Hutton, p. 312
Xunzi Feet that could walk the worldAnalogy A person has feet capable of walking anywhere, although nobody actually walks everywhere. The capacity to become a sage is distinct from realizing it: universal potential does not make achievement automatic. Xunzi 23Hutton, p. 317
Han Feizi Ministers carving and polishing themselvesMetaphor When a ruler reveals his desires, ministers reshape their presentation to match what he wants. Keeping preferences hidden makes flattery harder and helps the ruler assess ministers by their work. Han Feizi 5Harris, pp. 324–325
Han Feizi The ink line and crooked woodMetaphor A carpenter’s marking line stays straight instead of bending around crooked wood. Legal standards should not bend for noble status: powerful ministers face punishment, and commoners can earn rewards. Han Feizi 6Harris, pp. 332–333
Han Feizi Two handles; tiger’s claws and teethMetaphor A tiger surrendering its weapons to a dog becomes vulnerable. The ruler must retain control over rewards and punishments. Han Feizi 7Harris, p. 333
Han Feizi Cap keeper and robe keeperParable The cap keeper covers a sleeping ruler; both officials are punished. Officials must fulfill defined responsibilities without neglect or unauthorized interference. Han Feizi 7Harris, p. 335
Han Feizi Persuading someone who wants fame—or profitThought experiment An adviser tries promises of reputation or wealth on rulers with different public and private motives. Persuasion depends on understanding the hearer’s aims and self-image, not simply on knowing the facts or arguing clearly. Han Feizi 12Harris, pp. 336–338
Han Feizi Mi Zixia: borrowed carriage and half-eaten peachStory A ruler first praises Mi Zixia’s unauthorized carriage ride and shared peach, then condemns the same acts after affection fades. Personal favor can reverse judgments of unchanged conduct; advisers cannot assume that correctness protects them from a ruler’s changing feelings. Han Feizi 12Harris, pp. 339–340
Han Feizi The dragon’s inverted scalesAnalogy A dragon can be ridden, but touching the reversed scales beneath its throat provokes a deadly response. Persuaders must recognize a ruler’s dangerous sensitivities; access and familiarity do not guarantee safety. Han Feizi 12Harris, p. 340
Han FeiziFurther example Carriage makers and coffin makersAnalogy One hopes customers prosper; the other benefits when people die. Incentives shape conduct; apparent kindness or cruelty may reflect material interests. Han Feizi 17Liao, PDF p. 86
Han Feizi The landowner and hired field workersAnalogy A landowner offers good food and pay to secure diligent labor; workers labor hard to obtain those benefits. Aligned interests can sustain cooperation without affection, while frustrated expectations can produce resentment even between parents and children. Han Feizi 32Harbsmeier, vol. 2, pp. 562–563; hf 32.3.1
Han Feizi Spear and shieldParable A seller claims his shield stops everything and his spear pierces everything, but cannot say what happens when they meet. Claims of unstoppable moral worth and positional power that stops everything cannot both hold; the story exposes a contradiction in the chapter’s political debate. Han Feizi 40Harris, pp. 351–352
Han Feizi Dragons on clouds; snakes on mistMetaphor A flying dragon and winged snake lose their extraordinary powers when the clouds and mist supporting them disperse. Shen Dao, quoted here, uses this image to explain dependence on positional power; the chapter debates how far that claim can go. Han Feizi 40Harris, p. 348
Han Feizi Yao without office; Jie on the throneComparison The virtuous Yao as a commoner cannot command even a few people; the tyrant Jie can disrupt the world as ruler. Moral worth and power to command differ. Han Feizi’s reply advocates law-supported authority for ordinary rulers rather than dependence on exceptional sages. Han Feizi 40Harris, pp. 348, 351–352
Han Feizi Sons, daughters, and parental calculationExample Han Feizi describes parents celebrating a son’s birth but killing a daughter, explaining the contrast through expected future benefit. He argues that even family relations can involve calculations of advantage, challenging reliance on affection to secure political obedience. Han Feizi 46Harbsmeier, vol. 2, pp. 897–898; hf 46.3
Han Feizi The loving mother, teacher, and doctorAnalogy A loving mother sends a misbehaving child to a teacher and a seriously ill child to a doctor. Love alone does not supply the discipline or expertise needed to protect a child; Han Feizi questions making affection the basis of government. Han Feizi 47Harris, p. 356
Han Feizi Rabbit and tree stumpParable A farmer abandons farming after one rabbit accidentally dies against a tree. Policies must fit present conditions; copying ancient successes mechanically is foolish. Han Feizi 49Harris, p. 358
Han Feizi Nests and fire drills in the wrong ageHistorical analogy Sages invent shelters or ways to make fire for urgent needs; repeating those solutions in later ages would invite ridicule. Successful methods answer particular conditions. Their ancient prestige does not make them suitable for every later problem. Han Feizi 49Harris, p. 357
Han Feizi Five sons become twenty-five grandsonsThought experiment Five sons each have five sons; population expands while available goods become scarce. Han Feizi explains growing conflict through changing material conditions, challenging the assumption that ancient peace proves superior virtue. Han Feizi 49Harris, p. 358
Han Feizi Five vermin of the stateMetaphor Scholars, persuaders, private swordsmen, those evading battle, and merchants/artisans are compared to pests consuming the state. He condemns activities he presents as diverting effort or authority from law, farming, and warfare—a political judgment, not a neutral description of these groups. Han Feizi 49Harris, p. 371
Han Feizi Straight arrows and round wheelsAnalogy Craftspeople straighten arrow shafts and shape wheels instead of waiting for naturally perfect wood. Reliable laws, rewards, and punishments govern ordinary people; a state cannot depend on rare, spontaneous goodness. Han Feizi 50Harris, pp. 376–377
Han Feizi Lancing a child’s boilAnalogy A child is held still while its caring mother lances a boil. It cries at the pain, unable to understand the benefit. Han Feizi compares the people to the child: their resistance does not, he argues, disprove the benefit of harsh laws, taxes, or military service. Han Feizi 50Harris, p. 378
Han Feizi Dead founders and rival heirsThought experiment Eight Confucian branches and three Mohist branches disagree, yet each claims authenticity; their dead founders cannot settle the dispute. Claims of ancient authority need evidence. Invoking a founder does not resolve disagreement over what that founder actually taught. Han Feizi 50Harris, pp. 371–372
Han Feizi Frugal funeral or filial funeral?Comparison Rulers honor brief, inexpensive Mohist mourning as frugal and prolonged, costly Confucian mourning as filial. Han Feizi challenges rulers who praise incompatible standards without deciding which conduct they will actually reward. Han Feizi 50Harris, p. 372

Sources and reading notes

These are selected teaching paraphrases, not quotations or substitutes for the readings. “What it helps argue” is a concise interpretation. The chart includes thought experiments, metaphors, parables, and concrete comparisons; these categories overlap.

Read with the course packet: most passage links open the assigned translation. The page numbers below those links refer to the original books, not the packet’s PDF page count. “Further example” marks three retained examples outside the concentrated packets. Names vary by translation: Kjellberg’s Minnow and Breeze are Kun and Peng; his All-full is Hundun.

  • Course anthology: Philip J. Ivanhoe and Bryan W. Van Norden, eds., Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy, 3rd ed. (2023). Translators: Bryan W. Van Norden (Mengzi), Philip J. Ivanhoe (Laozi), Paul Kjellberg (Zhuangzi), Eric L. Hutton (Xunzi), and Eirik Lang Harris (Han Feizi).
  • Supplementary course sources: Irene Bloom, Mencius (2009); Eric L. Hutton, Xunzi: The Complete Text (2014); Christoph Harbsmeier, Han Feizi: The Art of Statecraft in Early China, vol. 2 (2025). Their names and page numbers identify passages added to the course packets.
  • Other translations: Van Norden’s selected Mengzi translations; Robert Eno’s complete Mengzi teaching translation; Eno’s Daodejing teaching translation; Zhuangzi at the Chinese Text Project; Columbia’s Xunzi on learning and human nature; W. K. Liao’s complete Han Feizi. Wording and pagination differ.

Compare carefully: Mengzi’s water tends downward; Laozi’s water is yielding and effective; Xunzi’s water can carry or overturn the ruler’s boat. Han Feizi’s arrows concern reliable institutions rather than naturally good subjects. His child-and-boil analogy defends coercive government; the chart reports that argument without endorsing it.

The butterfly dream and debate about fish invite competing interpretations. Cook Ding’s skill includes caution at difficult joints. Xunzi’s claim about bad human nature still allows everyone the capacity for moral improvement. Han Feizi’s spear-and-shield entry follows the assigned chapter 40 version; a related version appears in chapter 36.
