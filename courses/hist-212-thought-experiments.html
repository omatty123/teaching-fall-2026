Major thought experiments, metaphors, and parables in Mengzi, Laozi, Zhuangzi, Xunzi, and Han Feizi, with passage references.
ThinkerImage or experimentWhat happensWhat it helps arguePassage
Mengzi Child at the wellThought experiment Someone suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well and feels alarm and concern before calculating personal advantage. People have a spontaneous beginning of compassion; the example does not guarantee that everyone will carry out a rescue. Mengzi 2A6
Mengzi Four sproutsMetaphor Compassion, shame, deference, and moral judgment are compared to young sprouts. These beginnings of benevolence, righteousness, ritual propriety, and wisdom need cultivation to develop fully. Mengzi 2A6
Mengzi Pulling up seedlingsParable An impatient farmer pulls his seedlings upward to help them grow; they wither. Moral development needs patient cultivation: neither neglect growth nor force it. Mengzi 2A2
Mengzi King, ox, and sheepDialogue A king spares a frightened ox but substitutes a sheep he has not seen. Compassion can be real yet selective; the king should extend it to his people. Mengzi 1A7
Mengzi Water flowing downwardMetaphor Water naturally flows downward, although an external force can drive it upward. Human nature tends toward goodness; circumstances can divert that tendency. Mengzi 6A2
Mengzi Ox MountainMetaphor Logging and grazing strip a once-wooded mountain until it appears naturally barren. Damaged moral capacities do not show that human nature was originally bad; nourishment and conditions matter. Mengzi 6A8
Laozi WaterMetaphor Water nourishes everything, occupies low places, and overcomes what is hard despite its softness. Humility, non-contention, and flexibility can be more effective than force. Daodejing 8, 78
Laozi Empty hub, vessel, and roomMetaphor Wheels, pots, and rooms work because of their empty spaces. Absence and openness make useful activity possible; solidity alone is insufficient. Daodejing 11
Laozi Uncarved blockMetaphor Unworked wood represents simplicity before carving divides it into specialized objects. Preserve simplicity; avoid excessive desires and imposed distinctions. Daodejing 28, 32
Laozi InfantMetaphor A baby is physically soft yet holds firmly and embodies vitality and harmony. Genuine strength can coexist with softness and freedom from calculated striving. Daodejing 55
Laozi Cooking small fishMetaphor Governing a large state is compared to cooking delicate fish. A common reading: restrained government avoids damaging what it seeks to nourish through excessive handling. Daodejing 60
Zhuangzi Butterfly dreamThought experiment Zhou dreams he is a butterfly; awake, he wonders whether he is a butterfly dreaming he is Zhou. Questions certainty about identity and perspective; evokes the transformation of things. Zhuangzi 2
Zhuangzi Cook DingParable His knife follows the spaces within an ox’s joints, remaining sharp for years. At difficult joints, he slows down. Practiced responsiveness to a situation’s structure preserves both skill and life. Zhuangzi 3
Zhuangzi Useless treeParable A crooked tree escapes the carpenter and offers a place to rest. Conventional usefulness can endanger life; apparent uselessness can protect freedom. Zhuangzi 1
Zhuangzi Happiness of fishDialogue Huizi challenges Zhuangzi’s claim to know that swimming fish are happy. Explores how we know other beings’ experiences and the limits of argumentative certainty. Zhuangzi 17
Zhuangzi Hundun / ChaosParable Two rulers repay Hundun’s kindness by boring seven human openings into him; he dies. Imposing supposedly beneficial standards can destroy another being’s way of life. Zhuangzi 7
Zhuangzi Turtle in the mudParable Offered office, Zhuangzi asks whether a turtle would prefer honored bones or life in the mud. Living freely can be worth more than prestigious political service. Zhuangzi 17
Xunzi Crooked wood and dull metalMetaphor Wood needs straightening; metal needs sharpening. Teachers, ritual, and deliberate practice transform our untrained dispositions. Xunzi 23
Xunzi Blue dye and iceMetaphor Dye exceeds its plant’s color; ice is colder than water. Learning can transform people beyond their original condition. Xunzi 1
Xunzi Potter and clayMetaphor A potter deliberately makes a vessel from clay. Sages create ritual and moral standards through cultivated activity, rather than receiving them as spontaneous products of inborn nature. Xunzi 23
Xunzi Small steps and accumulating streamsMetaphor Long journeys require individual steps; rivers gather small streams. Moral achievement comes through accumulated, sustained practice. Xunzi 1
Han Feizi Two handles; tiger’s claws and teethMetaphor A tiger surrendering its weapons to a dog becomes vulnerable. The ruler must retain control over rewards and punishments. Han Feizi 7
Han Feizi Rabbit and tree stumpParable A farmer abandons farming after one rabbit accidentally dies against a tree. Policies must fit present conditions; copying ancient successes mechanically is foolish. Han Feizi 49
Han Feizi Spear and shieldParable A seller advertises an impenetrable shield and an all-piercing spear. Incompatible claims cannot both stand; Han Feizi challenges inconsistent political praise. Han Feizi 36
Han Feizi Cap keeper and robe keeperParable The cap keeper covers a sleeping ruler; both officials are punished. Officials must fulfill defined responsibilities without neglect or unauthorized interference. Han Feizi 7
Han Feizi Carriage makers and coffin makersAnalogy One hopes customers prosper; the other benefits when people die. Incentives shape conduct; apparent kindness or cruelty may reflect material interests. Han Feizi 17

Sources and reading notes

These entries are teaching paraphrases, not quotations. The final column links to source translations. Chapter titles and wording vary by edition; references identify the received texts.

  • Mengzi: Bryan W. Van Norden’s selected translations (1997) for 1A7, 2A2, and 2A6; James Legge’s translation at the Chinese Text Project for 6A2 and 6A8. A and B denote the two halves of a book.
  • Laozi / Daodejing: James Legge’s translation (1891), with Robert Eno’s teaching translation, version 1.4 (2010–2025), for the uncarved block. The advice to avoid overhandling small fish explains the metaphor; the chapter itself gives the comparison without spelling out that instruction.
  • Zhuangzi: James Legge’s translation at the Chinese Text Project. The butterfly and fish episodes invite competing interpretations; they do not simply prove that reality is illusion or settle the question of other minds. Cook Ding’s skill includes caution. This useless-tree entry follows chapter 1; the dream of Carpenter Shi and the tree is a different episode in chapter 4.
  • Xunzi: “Encouraging Learning” and “Human Nature Is Evil”, from Sources of Chinese Tradition, compiled by Wm. Theodore de Bary and Irene Bloom, 2nd ed., vol. 1 (1999), hosted by Columbia University’s Asia for Educators. These excerpts do not identify individual translators. The latter title does not mean people cannot become good: transformation through learning is central to the argument.
  • Han Feizi: W. K. Liao, The Complete Works of Han Fei Tzŭ. Passage links include PDF page locations where supported. Liao calls the weapon in the familiar spear-and-shield story a halberd. The cap-and-robe episode reports both officials being punished without resolving differences in how translators describe the penalties.

Compare carefully: water makes different arguments in Mengzi and Laozi. Mengzi emphasizes its natural direction; Laozi emphasizes its humility, softness, and efficacy.
