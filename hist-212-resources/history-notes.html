Three Sovereigns 三皇

The list in Sima Zhen’s later supplement to the Shiji.

Primary source

Sima Zhen: Supplementary Annals of the Three Sovereigns Chinese original

Read in English

Sima Zhen (Tang), Supplementary Annals of the Three Sovereigns · excerpts on Fuxi, Nüwa and Shennong

Taihao Paoxi (Fuxi) had the surname Feng. He succeeded Suiren and ruled as Heaven’s heir. His mother, named Huaxu, stepped in the footprint of a giant at Leize and bore Paoxi at Chengji. He had a snake’s body and a human head, and possessed sage-like virtue. Looking up, he observed the images in Heaven; looking down, he observed the patterns of the Earth. He studied the markings of birds and beasts and what suited the land, taking models near at hand from his own body and far off from other things. He first drew the eight trigrams, to communicate with the virtue of the spirits and to sort out the nature of the myriad things. He made written tallies to replace government by knotted cords. He then first set up marriage, with paired deerskins as the gift. He tied nets and snares and taught fishing and hunting; hence he is called Fuxi.

Nüwa likewise had the surname Feng, with a snake’s body and a human head, and divine, sage-like virtue. She succeeded Fuxi and was called Nüxi. She made no reforms; she only made the sheng and huang (reed pipes).

Yandi Shennong had the surname Jiang. His mother, named Nüdeng, a daughter of the You Jiao clan and a consort of Shaodian, was moved by a divine dragon and bore Yandi, who had a human body and an ox’s head. He grew up by the Jiang River and took his surname from it. He ruled through the virtue of Fire, and so was called Yandi (“Flame Emperor”); he named his officials after fire. He hewed wood into plowshares and bent wood into plow handles, and taught everyone the use of the plow and hoe. Because he first taught farming, he was called Shennong (“Divine Farmer”). He then made the zha sacrifice, and with a red whip he lashed the plants and trees. He first tasted the hundred herbs, and so medicine began. He also made a five-stringed zither.

Five Emperors 五帝

Yao 堯 · Shun 舜Fourth and fifth, just below

The sequence in Sima Qian’s Shiji. These are traditional accounts of remote antiquity.

Primary source

Shiji 1: Five Emperors Chinese original

Read in English

Sima Qian, Shiji 1, Annals of the Five Emperors · the opening lines on each

The Yellow Emperor was a son of Shaodian. His surname was Gongsun and his personal name Xuanyuan. He was divinely gifted from birth; as an infant he could speak; as a child he was quick and attentive; grown, he was earnest and sharp; and as an adult he was wise and clear-sighted.

Emperor Zhuanxu was a grandson of the Yellow Emperor and a son of Changyi. He was calm and deep, and so capable of planning; he was open-minded and understood affairs.

Emperor Ku was a great-grandson of the Yellow Emperor.

Emperor Yao was named Fangxun. His benevolence was like Heaven’s and his wisdom like a spirit’s. To approach him was like approaching the sun; to gaze at him was like looking at clouds. He was rich without arrogance and eminent without laxity.

Shun of Yu was named Chonghua. (…) Shun’s father Gusou was blind, and Shun’s mother died; Gusou took another wife and had a son, Xiang, who was arrogant. Gusou loved his second wife’s son and often wanted to kill Shun. Shun fled and hid, and if he committed a small fault he accepted the punishment. He served his father, stepmother and brother with a sincerity and care that grew each day, and never slackened.

Shiji 2: Xia Chinese original

Read in English

Sima Qian, Shiji 2, Annals of Xia · Yu’s descent, and the last ruler

Yu of Xia was named Wenming. Yu’s father was Gun; Gun’s father was Emperor Zhuanxu; Zhuanxu’s father was Changyi; and Changyi’s father was the Yellow Emperor. Yu was thus a great-great-grandson of the Yellow Emperor and a grandson of Emperor Zhuanxu.

In the time of Emperor Jie, from Kong Jia on, many of the lords had turned away from Xia. Jie did not devote himself to virtue but used force to harm the people, and the people could not endure it. He then summoned Tang and imprisoned him at Xiatai, but soon released him. Tang cultivated virtue and the lords all turned to him, and Tang then led troops to attack Jie of Xia. Jie fled to Mingtiao, was banished, and died. Jie said to someone, “I regret that I did not kill Tang at Xiatai, and so brought myself to this.” Tang then took the seat of the Son of Heaven, replacing Xia and holding court over the world.

This passage, and the rest of Sima Qian’s Xia annals, does not mention Mo Xi or a wine pool. That story appears later.

Mo Xi 妺喜

The Lienüzhuan pairs Jie and Mo Xi with a wine lake and debauchery.

Read Lienüzhuan 7 · first biography Chinese original

Read in English

Liu Xiang, Lienüzhuan 7, “Mo Xi, consort of Jie of Xia” · excerpt

Mo Xi was the consort of Jie of Xia. She was beautiful to look at but thin in virtue; disorderly, wicked and lawless, a woman with a man’s heart, who wore a sword and a cap. Jie, having cast aside ritual and righteousness, was lewd with women. He sought out beautiful women and stored them in the rear palace, and gathered singers, entertainers, dwarfs and favorites who could put on strange and marvelous shows, and kept them at his side, making licentious music. Day and night he drank with Mo Xi and the palace women, without ever resting. He set Mo Xi on his knee and listened to and followed her words. Confused and off the Way, he was arrogant, extravagant and self-indulgent.

He made a pool of wine large enough to row a boat on. At one beat of the drum, three thousand men drank from it like oxen, lowering their heads to the pool of wine; those who grew drunk and drowned made Mo Xi laugh, and she took it as amusement. Long Pang came forward to remonstrate: “My lord, you are without the Way, and you will surely perish.” Jie said, “Does the sun perish? When the sun perishes, I shall perish.” He would not listen; he took it for ill-omened talk and killed him.

Written in the Han period, long after the traditional date of these events, in a book of moral examples about women. Compare the note on the Shiji above.

Da Ji 妲己

The Shiji and Lienüzhuan associate this court with a wine lake and a meat forest.

Read Shiji 3 · King Zhòu and Da Ji Chinese original

Read in English

Sima Qian, Shiji 3, Annals of Yin · King Zhòu and Da Ji

[Zhòu] was fond of wine and lewd pleasures and doted on women. He loved Da Ji and followed whatever Da Ji said. He had the music master Juan compose new licentious sounds, the dances of Beili, and decadent music. He levied heavy taxes to fill the treasury at Deer Terrace and the granary at Juqiao with grain. He gathered more dogs, horses and rare objects, until the palaces were crammed with them. He enlarged the parks and terraces at Shaqiu and put many wild beasts and flying birds inside. He was contemptuous of ghosts and spirits. He gathered a great crowd for amusement at Shaqiu. He made a pool of wine and hung meat to make a forest, and had men and women chase one another naked among them, holding drinking bouts through the long night.

According to Yiqun Zhou (see the notes below), Sima Qian is the first author to name Da Ji as Zhòu’s accomplice. The Shangshu’s Speech at Mu blames “a woman” without naming her.

Da Ji was the consort of Zhòu of Yin, and was greatly favored by him. (…) He was fond of wine and lewd pleasures and never left Da Ji’s side; whomever Da Ji praised he honored, and whomever she hated he had executed. He made new licentious music, the dances of the northern borderland, and decadent songs; he collected rare things and stored them in the rear palace, so that flatterers and the many women all got what they wanted. He piled up brewers’ dregs into a hill, let wine flow into a pool, and hung meat as a forest, and had people chase one another naked among them, drinking through the long night. Bao Si 褒姒

The “cried wolf” story: King You repeatedly signals a false alarm to make her laugh.

Read Shiji 4 · King You and Bao Si Chinese original

Read in English

Sima Qian, Shiji 4, Annals of Zhou · King You and Bao Si

Bao Si did not like to laugh. King You tried every means to make her laugh, but she still did not. King You had set up beacon fires and great drums, so that if raiders came the beacons would be lit. The lords all arrived, but there were no raiders, and Bao Si laughed loudly. King You was pleased, and lit the beacons many times. Afterwards the lords no longer trusted them, and came less and less.

(…) The Marquis of Shen was angered and, with Zeng and the Western Yi and the Quanrong, attacked King You. King You lit the beacons to summon troops, but no troops came. So King You was killed at the foot of Mount Li, Bao Si was captured, and the attackers took all of Zhou’s wealth and left.

Why is Shun recommended?

In the Book of Documents, Yao asks for a successor. Shun’s standing rests on his conduct toward his family.

“Noble son” in the notes is best understood as filial virtue. The passage identifies Shun as a man of low social standing.

“He has been able (however), by his filial piety to live in harmony with them…”

Shangshu / Shujing · “Canon of Yao” · James Legge translation

Read the full passage

The Di said, 'Ho! (President of) the Four Mountains, I have been on the throne seventy years. You can carry out my commands - I will resign my place to you.'

The Chief said, 'I have not the virtue; I should disgrace your place.' (The Di) said, 'Show me some one among the illustrious, or set forth one from among the poor and mean.'

All (then) said to the Di, 'There is an unmarried man among the lower people, called Shun of Yu'. The Di said, 'Yes, I have heard of him. What have you to say about him?'

The Chief said,' He is the son of a blind man. His father was obstinately unprincipled; his (step-)mother was insincere; his (half-) brother Xiang was arrogant.

He has been able (however), by his filial piety to live in harmony with them, and to lead them gradually to self-government, so that they (no longer) proceed to great wickedness.'

The Di said, 'I will try him; I will wive him, and thereby see his behaviour with my two daughters.' (Accordingly) he arranged and sent down his two daughters to the north of the Gui,

to be wives in (the family of) Yu. The Di said to them, 'Be reverent!'

Source notes and corrections

Dates, king counts, and corrected labels

“Mingtao” is corrected to Mingtiao. “Destroyer” and “destoyer” become final ruler. King Wu overthrew the last Shang king; he did not overthrow the Zhou dynasty.

The sheet’s counts—17 Xia, 30 Shang, and about 13 Western Zhou kings—are preserved as traditional study notes. The Western Zhou number depends on the counting convention; including King Wen is different from counting kings from Wu onward. The sheet does not specify its convention.

Xia’s dates are traditional, not securely established. The Neolithic image’s c. 8500–2070 BCE range is retained in the original below, but Neolithic chronology varies by region. All periods are schematic here.

Three Sovereigns: an attribution correction

The embedded table attributes its material to an edition by Sima Qian. Sima Qian’s Shiji begins with the Five Emperors. The Three Sovereigns annals were a later addition by Sima Zhen.

Fu Xi, Nüwa, and Shennong form the list used above. Heavenly Sovereign, Earthly Sovereign, and Tai Sovereign appear as another set in the source image; the table’s “or” pairings should not be treated as universal equivalences.

Reference: Yoshihiro Watanabe, “On the Characteristics of the Three Traditional Commentaries on the Shiji” (English abstract on the final page).

Consorts and accounts of dynastic collapse

The pairings and story labels are retained from the notes. Their explanatory status is qualified: later authors shaped these accounts to make moral and political arguments.

Bao Si’s story is a particularly clear example. Accounts emphasize different causes, including King You’s ministers, succession dispute, and misuse of alarm signals. Blaming a consort alone obscures those differences.
