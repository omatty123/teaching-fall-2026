Politics

Who won the Great Debate—and why didn’t the losers disappear?Winning a court’s support, shaping its laws, and changing everyday life are different kinds of victory.

Key figures

Kongzi and Han Feizi offer rival methods of rule. Zhu Xi makes Ru learning a program for schools and households. Mo, Dao, and Fo give you other cases of survival to investigate.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Analects · 2.3
    Virtue and ritual versus punishment.
  • Han Feizi · ch. 7, “The Two Handles,” p. 333
    The ruler’s control of rewards and punishments.

For November 4: Compare Ru and Fa first. Give a tentative answer about what each offered a ruler; later evidence must establish which methods were adopted.

Why wasn’t there another Qin—or was there?A dynasty can collapse without taking its institutions or ambitions with it.

Key figures

Li Si defends centralized power. Lu Jia reconsiders the methods that built an empire after Qin’s fall.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Li Si’s memorials · SCT 207–212
    Centralized rule and control of political argument.
  • Lu Jia, New Discourses · opening selection in the Han philosophy packet
    The difference between acquiring an empire and governing it.

For November 4: Identify one Qin method and test whether Lu Jia rejects it, modifies it, or leaves it intact.

How could Korea and Japan borrow China’s institutions without accepting China’s rule?Borrowing another court’s language and institutions could help a ruler claim authority of their own.

Key figures

King Sinmun answers a Tang demand about a royal name. The Japanese reformers use registers and land policy to strengthen their court.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Silla’s reply to the Tang emperor · SKT 64–65
    Deference in language and resistance in the result.
  • Taika reform edicts · SJT 77–79
    Local rulers and powerful households face new claims from the central court.

For November 4: Compare one act of diplomatic resistance with one institutional reform. Specify what is borrowed and who gains authority.

Culture

Why did rulers need monks—and sometimes fear them?Buddhist protection could strengthen a dynasty, while monasteries created rival claims on loyalty and resources.

Key figures

Huiyuan defends monks’ independence from royal ceremony. Wang Geon seeks Buddhist protection but warns against uncontrolled temple building.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Huiyuan, “A Monk Does Not Bow Down Before a King” · SCT 427–429
    The claim that monastic life changes obligations to rulers.
  • Wang Geon’s Ten Injunctions · nos. 1–2, SKT 154
    Royal support for Buddhism and limits on its institutions.

For November 4: Test whether royal patronage and royal control were partners or competing goals.

Could a woman influence politics without holding office?Rank, marriage, motherhood, and cultural authority create kinds of power that an official title does not capture.

Key figures

Murasaki Shikibu makes a court woman’s status central to Genji’s opening. Zhu Xi’s family rites organize authority within the household.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • The Tale of Genji · ch. 1, “The Paulownia Pavilion” / “Paulownia Court”
    The emperor’s favor and the position of Genji’s mother.
  • Zhu Xi’s Ancestral Rites · CC 157–163
    Roles and rank within the family’s ritual life.

For November 4: Compare one scene of influence with one prescribed household role. Treat the novel and the ritual manual as different kinds of evidence.

What ancient ideas still live on today—and who kept them alive?A teaching can survive through families, schools, religious communities, or a later revival. It can also change along the way.

Key figures

Laozi presents nonaction as a way to govern. Han Feizi makes a ruler’s nonaction part of a method for controlling ministers.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Laozi · 37, p. 189
    Nonaction and the possibility that people will order their own lives.
  • Han Feizi · ch. 5, “The Way of the Ruler,” pp. 324–325
    The ruler conceals intentions while ministers carry out their work.

For November 4: Begin with one idea: nonaction. Explain how its meaning changes between these texts. To claim it survives today, trace its history to a specific modern practice or revival.

War

Why did rulers celebrate wars that Mozi condemned?The same violence can appear as a ruler’s achievement or as an attack on other people’s lives and livelihoods.

Key figures

Mozi condemns aggressive war. The minister in the Salt and Iron Debate defends the revenues used for frontier warfare.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Mozi · ch. 17, p. 73
    The contrast between condemning private wrongdoing and praising attacks on states.
  • The Debate on Salt and Iron · CC 61
    The minister’s defense of military expenditure.

For November 4: Compare the standards used to justify or condemn war. Distinguish aggression from the minister’s claim of defense.

How did Buddhists justify fighting wars?The prohibition on killing meets the demands of a king and the obligations of a warrior.

Key figures

Wŏn’gwang writes a royal war petition and advises lay warriors. Huiyuan offers a different account of monks’ obligations to rulers.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Wŏn’gwang’s war petition and commandments · SKT 45–46
    Service to the king, battlefield loyalty, and limits on killing.
  • Huiyuan on monks and rulers · SCT 427–429
    The distinction between householders and those who leave household life.

For November 4: Explain how status and political circumstances change the obligations in these passages. Do not treat all Buddhists as one voice.

When is rebellion the right thing to do?If a ruler becomes a tyrant, obedience and moral duty may point in opposite directions.

Key figures

Mengzi denies a tyrant the moral status of a ruler. Han Feizi warns rulers against surrendering power to ministers.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • Mengzi · 1B8, p. 128
    The distinction between killing a ruler and executing a tyrant.
  • Han Feizi · ch. 7, p. 333
    Zi Han’s acquisition of the ruler’s power to punish.

For November 4: Compare who is entitled to judge a ruler and what makes a transfer of power legitimate or dangerous.

Economics

Salt and Iron: should the state run the economy?Public revenue, military security, private profit, and ordinary livelihoods pull policy in different directions.

Key figures

The minister and the learned men disagree about state monopolies and what a government should value.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • The Debate on Salt and Iron: the minister · CC 61
    The case for revenues that sustain frontier defense.
  • The Debate on Salt and Iron: the learned men · CC 61
    The challenge to profit-seeking government.

For November 4: Use one passage from each side and explain whose interests each claims to protect. These are two passages from one primary source.

Does a strong state need weak people?A government’s wealth and power may depend on limiting the choices available to its subjects.

Key figures

The Book of Lord Shang makes the relationship between state strength and popular weakness explicit. Mengzi links rule to people’s livelihoods.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • The Book of Lord Shang · 20.1
    The claim that a strong people means a weak state.
  • Mengzi · 1A7
    Secure livelihoods and the ruler’s claim to allegiance.

For November 4: Explain what “strong” and “weak” mean in each argument. Test whether the two authors agree on whose welfare government should serve.

Why did rulers call farming essential and commerce dangerous?The freedom to make a living outside agriculture could conflict with the state’s demands for food, labor, and soldiers.

Key figures

The Book of Lord Shang directs ambition toward farming and warfare. The Salt and Iron debaters dispute commerce, profit, and the people’s work.

Key texts · two starting passages

  • The Book of Lord Shang · 3.2, p. 134
    Agriculture and warfare as the approved routes to status.
  • The Debate on Salt and Iron · CC 61
    Arguments about agriculture, trade, and state revenue.

For November 4: Compare the reasons for directing people’s work. Explain whether the stated goal is popular prosperity, moral order, or state power.
