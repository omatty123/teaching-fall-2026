Politics

Why wasn’t there another Qin—or was there?

Argument to test: Dynasties could reject Qin’s reputation while retaining powerful institutions. Test what survived, what changed, and whether later rulers repeated its mistakes.

Course sources: China: Li Si’s memorials and Jia Yi’s criticism of Qin. Korea: Wang Geon’s Ten Injunctions. Japan: Taika reform edicts. Compare central control, advice, and demands on the population.

Present connection to research: State power, administrative uniformity, and limits on rulers.

What makes a ruler legitimate: birth, force, virtue, or results?

Argument to test: Winning power and justifying power are different tasks. Compare the reasons rulers gave for deserving obedience.

Course sources: China: Covenant of Tang and Hongwu’s proclamations. Korea: the Joseon founding edict. Japan: Shōtoku’s Seventeen-Article Constitution. Hongwu and Joseon are later course readings.

Present connection to research: The reasons modern governments give for their authority.

Can a ruler be too powerful to govern well?

Argument to test: Concentrated authority can make decisions faster while making mistakes harder to correct. Test the role of criticism and officials.

Course sources: China: Han Feizi and Taizong’s advice on accepting remonstrance. Korea: Wang Geon’s injunction 7. Japan: the Seventeen-Article Constitution.

Present connection to research: Advice, dissent, accountability, and restraints on leaders.

Can you copy another country’s government without becoming like it?

Argument to test: Borrowing institutions can strengthen local rulers without erasing local identities. Trace what was adopted, altered, or refused.

Course sources: China: Taizong’s Plan for an Emperor. Korea: Silla’s response over the title Taizong and Wang Geon’s Ten Injunctions. Japan: Taika reform edicts.

Present connection to research: Borrowed institutions and claims of national distinctiveness.

Culture

What ancient ideas still live on now?

Argument to test: Choose one idea—filial duty, merit, loyalty, harmony, or moral leadership—and trace both its survival and its changes. Do not treat all of East Asia as one culture.

Course sources: For filial duty: China, Analects and the assigned Mouzi passages; Korea, Wŏn’gwang’s Five Commandments; Japan, the Seventeen-Article Constitution. Use contrasting passages, not just matching vocabulary.

Present connection to research: One documented present-day practice or statement using the chosen idea. Research the intervening history before claiming inheritance.

Does religion change society, or does society change religion?

Argument to test: A religion can change as it gains rulers, patrons, and new audiences. Test what those changes make possible and what they compromise.

Course sources: China: Mouzi and Huiyuan. Korea: Wŏn’gwang and Wang Geon’s injunctions. Japan: Shōmu’s Great Buddha proclamation. Use at least four distinct primary texts.

Present connection to research: Religious authority, political sponsorship, and public obligations.

Who gets to count as educated—and what does education get them?

Argument to test: Education can challenge inherited rank and also help elites preserve it. Compare claims about learning with evidence of access and reward.

Course sources: China: Analects and Su Shi and the Song students. Korea: Kangsu’s biography. Japan: The Tale of Genji. Treat the novel as a representation of courtly accomplishment, not an enrollment record.

Present connection to research: Credentials, social status, and unequal access to education.

Who gets to define a good woman?

Argument to test: Prescriptions for women can serve households and political orders; literary representations can reveal tensions those prescriptions conceal.

Course sources: China: assigned Song women selections and Empress Xu’s Instructions. Korea: The Story of Hong Gildong, for the effects of a mother’s status. Japan: The Tale of Genji. Empress Xu and Hong Gildong are later readings; distinguish prescription from fiction.

Present connection to research: Family roles, status, and who has authority to prescribe them.

War

Can a war be just?

Argument to test: Calling a war defensive, punitive, or benevolent does not settle whether it is justified. Compare the stated reasons with the interests and costs involved.

Course sources: China: Mozi and the Salt and Iron debate. Korea: Wŏn’gwang’s war petition account. Japan: The Way of the Warrior. The Japanese readings come later in the course.

Present connection to research: The language used to justify war; compare a specific modern statement without assuming direct inheritance.

Who pays for a powerful state?

Argument to test: Military strength can protect some people while imposing heavy costs on others. Follow taxes, labor, service, and displacement.

Course sources: China: Lord Shang and the Salt and Iron debate. Korea: Relations with the Mongols. Japan: Taika reform edicts. Distinguish promised protection from evidence of actual burdens.

Present connection to research: Defense spending and the distribution of its costs.

Is resistance always the right choice?

Argument to test: Resistance can preserve autonomy but impose costs on people who did not choose it. Test how rulers and subjects describe that choice differently.

Course sources: China: Chinggis Khan’s letter to Changchun and A Mongol Governor. Korea: Relations with the Mongols. Japan: The Way of the Warrior. These are later course sources; use earlier political arguments as a second period.

Present connection to research: Surrender, compromise, and responsibility for civilian lives.

Does war build civilizations or destroy them?

Argument to test: Conquest can move people, skills, and institutions while destroying lives and livelihoods. A strong answer must account for both without treating suffering as an automatic price of progress.

Course sources: China: Li Si’s memorials and Chinggis Khan’s letter. Korea: Relations with the Mongols. Japan: The Way of the Warrior. Use Ebrey and Morgan for context; they do not count as primary sources.

Present connection to research: Claims that violence produces order, unity, or development.

Economics

Salt and Iron: should the government run the economy?

Argument to test: Public control can fund shared needs and restrain private power; it can also give officials new ways to exploit people. Judge the proposed controls by whom they benefit.

Course sources: China: the Salt and Iron debate and Lord Shang. Korea: Wang Geon’s injunctions 7 and 9. Japan: Taika reform edicts. The two sides of Salt and Iron are passages within one primary text, not two independent sources.

Present connection to research: One modern debate over public control of an essential good or service. Compare the problem; do not label the ancient speakers capitalist or socialist.

What does a government owe its people—and what can it demand in return?

Argument to test: Protection does not justify unlimited demands. Compare the claimed benefits of rule with limits on taxation, labor, and obedience.

Course sources: China: the Salt and Iron debate and Mengzi. Korea: Wang Geon’s Ten Injunctions. Japan: Taika reform edicts or the Nintoku account. Use the later court history carefully when discussing Nintoku.

Present connection to research: Taxes, public services, and the duties of government.

Why did rulers need merchants but distrust them?

Argument to test: Trade can supply governments and households while concentrating wealth beyond official control. Test whether attacks on merchants target greed, independence, or competition with the state.

Course sources: China: the Salt and Iron debate and the assigned Song city selections. Korea: Wang Geon’s injunction 8 on occupation and access to office. Japan: Taika edicts on property and powerful households. The Korean and Japanese passages broaden the comparison to wealth and status; they are not direct descriptions of merchant life.

Present connection to research: Wealth, political influence, and regulation.

Does prosperity make a society fairer?

Argument to test: A richer society can offer more opportunities while retaining or deepening inequalities. Separate increased wealth from its distribution.

Course sources: China: Salt and Iron and the assigned Song city and women selections. Korea: Wang Geon’s injunctions on labor, status, and salaries. Japan: The Tale of Genji and Taika reform edicts. Compare who benefits and who remains dependent.

Present connection to research: Economic growth, inequality, and social mobility.
