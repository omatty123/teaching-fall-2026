The Pensive Bodhisattva

Korea · Three Kingdoms period · early 7th century

A figure in thought

One leg crosses the other. Fingers touch the cheek. A small crown rests above a nearly bare upper body.

Bodhisattva
A being on the path to Buddhahood; in Mahāyāna Buddhism, the ideal joins wisdom with compassion for other beings.
Maitreya · Mireuk 미륵
The future Buddha. Korean pensive images are often associated with Maitreya.
Gilt bronze
A bronze sculpture with a thin covering of gold.

The object is in Seoul today. Its precise original temple and kingdom are uncertain; “Korean” does not by itself establish “Silla.”

Mouzi: defending Buddhism with Chinese authorities

Mouzi 牟子 · “Master Mou” · Disposing of Error, SCT pp. 421–426

牟子

Mouzi · Móuzǐ

The voice of a dialogue defending Buddhism against objections about the Classics, family duties, and foreign origins. The text presents a late-Han setting; its authorship and date remain debated.

Mouzi 牟子 is not Mozi 墨子, the earlier thinker studied in this course. Text and attribution.

Filial piety · xiào 孝

Duties to parents and ancestors. The objections concern preserving the body received from parents and continuing the family line.

Leaving the household · chūjiā 出家

Becoming a monk or nun changes marriage, property, appearance, and family obligations.

The questioner

“But now the monks shave their heads. How this violates the sayings of the sages and is out of keeping with the way of the filial!”

SCT p. 423. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

Mouzi

“Taibo cut his hair short and tattooed his body ... And yet Confucius praised him, saying that his might well be called the ultimate virtue.”

SCT p. 423. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

The questioner

“Now of felicities there is none greater than the continuation of one’s line, of unfilial conduct there is none worse than childlessness.”

SCT pp. 423–424. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

Mouzi

“Boyi and Shuqi starved in Shouyang, but Confucius praised their worth. ... One does not hear of their being ill-spoken of because they were childless and propertyless.”

SCT p. 424. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

Mouzi

“The spirit never perishes. Only the body decays.”

SCT p. 424 · compare the editors on p. 416: no eternal Self or soul. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

The names inside Mouzi’s replies
Taibo
An exemplary figure associated with yielding succession and adopting the customs of Wu. Mouzi invokes Confucius’s praise despite Taibo’s cut hair and tattoos.
Boyi and Shuqi
Brothers remembered for refusing to serve the Zhou and starving at Shouyang. Mouzi uses their honored withdrawal as a precedent.
Bian Que
A famous physician. Mouzi’s medical analogy allows more than one source of effective remedies.

SCT pp. 421–424. These are precedents in Mouzi’s argument, not evidence that those figures were Buddhists.

Huiyuan: monks and royal authority

Huiyuan, A Monk Does Not Bow Down Before a King · SCT pp. 427–429

Huiyuan 慧遠 · Huìyuǎn

334–416 · Mount Lu, southern China

A monk and community leader at Donglin Temple. His A Monk Does Not Bow Down Before a King dates to 404, during the controversy over Huan Xuan’s demand for monastic obeisance.

His distinction: householders follow ordinary family and political duties; monastics serve a religious purpose that warrants a different ritual status.

Biographical background · SCT pp. 427–429.

Those who stay in the household

“Therefore they who rejoice in the way of Śākya invariably first serve their parents and respect their lords.”

SCT p. 427. Oct 13 reader · p. 4

Those who leave it

“Kings and princes, though they have the power of preserving existence, cannot cause a preserved creature to be without woe. ... This is why the monk refuses homage to the Lord of the Myriad Chariots”

SCT pp. 428–429. Oct 13 reader · p. 4

Silla: conversion and royal patronage

Sources of Korean Tradition · pp. 41–46

Pŏphŭng · Beopheung

The king associated with Silla’s official acceptance of Buddhism. The reading calls him Pŏpkong, his monastic name.

Ich’adon

The court official whose execution and miraculous death mark the conversion account, dated 527.

A later sacred biography

The account survives in Kakhun’s Lives of Eminent Korean Monks (1215). Miracles are the narrative’s claims.

The ministers object

“In recent years the crops have been scarce, and the people are restless. Besides, because of frequent border raids from the neighboring state, our soldiers are still engaged in battle.”

SKT pp. 41–42. Oct 13 reader · p. 6

Ich’adon

“Sacrificing his life in order to accomplish goodness is the great principle of the official.”

SKT p. 42. Oct 13 reader · p. 6

Wŏn’gwang and the petition for troops

Wŏn’gwang 圓光 · Wongwang in modern romanization · Silla monk and royal adviser

걸사표 乞師表

Geolsapyo · Petition Requesting Troops

Requested by: King Chinp’yŏng of Silla.
Addressed to: the Sui court.
Purpose: military help against Koguryŏ.

The petition’s original wording does not survive. The passage below records the monk’s reply to his king, not the text sent to Sui.

  1. 608The king commissions Wŏn’gwang to draft it.
  2. 611Silla’s embassy requests troops, according to the historical account.
  3. 612Sui launches its major invasion of Koguryŏ. Do not reduce the war’s causes to this petition.

Academy of Korean Studies: petition, dates, and lost text · Samguk sagi: the 608 entry.

Wŏn’gwang’s reported reply to the king

“To destroy others in order to preserve oneself is not the way of a monk. But since I, a poor monk, live in Your Majesty’s territory and waste Your Majesty’s clothes and food, I dare not disobey.”

SKT p. 45. Oct 13 reader · p. 7

The tension is explicit: the monk opposes destroying others, yet acknowledges his dependence on the king’s territory and supplies.

Five Commandments for Laymen

“serve your sovereign with loyalty; attend your parents with filial piety; treat your friends with sincerity; do not retreat from a battlefield; be discriminating about the taking of life.”

SKT pp. 45–46. Oct 13 reader · p. 7

Names and institutions in the rest of the Silla reading
Chajang · Jajang 자장
A monk who studied in Tang China and became Silla’s national monastic overseer. SKT pp. 46–48.
Hwarang 화랑 · “flower youths”
Silla youth groups associated with elite cultivation, royal recruitment, and military service. SKT pp. 54–55.
Kwanch’ang · Gwanchang 관창
The young hwarang whose death in battle becomes an example of loyalty and courage. SKT pp. 55–56.
Chin’gol · jingol 진골 · “true bone”
A hereditary elite rank in Silla’s status hierarchy. Chajang’s biography names this background.

Wang Xizhi: handwriting and printed type

Wang Xizhi 王羲之 · Wáng Xīzhī · fourth-century Eastern Jin · Ebrey pp. 64, 70

An aristocrat, official, and celebrated calligrapher. Ebrey describes copying his work as a way to train the hand. The example here is a later modelbook reproduction of Timely Clearing After Snowfall, not Wang’s original sheet.

The same four characters in print

快雪時晴 · Kuài xuě shí qíng
“Clearing after sudden snow.”

Select a character to locate it in the model.

The model uses running script (xíngshū): strokes vary and movement links their forms. The printed characters use modern sans-serif type.

Five-minute calligraphy comparison

  1. Copy the four printed characters once.
  2. Copy their forms from the historical model.
  3. Circle differences in spacing, proportion, and connections.

Pen or pencil is enough for this comparison. A brush produces different changes in line width.
