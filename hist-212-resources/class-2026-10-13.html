Mouzi: defending Buddhism with Chinese authorities

Mouzi 牟子 · “Master Mou” · Disposing of Error, SCT pp. 421–426

牟子

Mouzi · Móuzǐ

The voice of a dialogue defending Buddhism against objections about the Classics, family duties, and foreign origins. The text presents a late-Han setting; its authorship and date remain debated.

Mouzi 牟子 is not Mozi 墨子, the earlier thinker studied in this course. Text and attribution.

Filial piety · xiào 孝

Duties to parents and ancestors. The objections concern preserving the body received from parents and continuing the family line.

Leaving the household · chūjiā 出家

Becoming a monk or nun changes marriage, property, appearance, and family obligations.

The questioner

“But now the monks shave their heads. How this violates the sayings of the sages and is out of keeping with the way of the filial!”

SCT p. 423. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

Mouzi

“Taibo cut his hair short and tattooed his body ... And yet Confucius praised him, saying that his might well be called the ultimate virtue.”

SCT p. 423. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

The questioner

“Now of felicities there is none greater than the continuation of one’s line, of unfilial conduct there is none worse than childlessness.”

SCT pp. 423–424. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

Mouzi

“Boyi and Shuqi starved in Shouyang, but Confucius praised their worth. ... One does not hear of their being ill-spoken of because they were childless and propertyless.”

SCT p. 424. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

Mouzi

“The spirit never perishes. Only the body decays.”

SCT p. 424 · compare the editors on p. 416: no eternal Self or soul. Oct 13 reader · p. 3

The names inside Mouzi’s replies
Taibo
An exemplary figure associated with yielding succession and adopting the customs of Wu. Mouzi invokes Confucius’s praise despite Taibo’s cut hair and tattoos.
Boyi and Shuqi
Brothers remembered for refusing to serve the Zhou and starving at Shouyang. Mouzi uses their honored withdrawal as a precedent.
Bian Que
A famous physician. Mouzi’s medical analogy allows more than one source of effective remedies.

SCT pp. 421–424. These are precedents in Mouzi’s argument, not evidence that those figures were Buddhists.

Gentlemen and “barbarian” practices: PRO / CON

Mouzi’s debate · earlier precedent: the king of Zhao’s foreign dress

PRO: reasons to engage with foreign people, teachings, or practices. CON: reasons to resist. The sources address different actions: living among foreigners, learning from them, and adopting their dress.

Living among foreigners

PRO · Mouzi cites Confucius

“If a noble person dwells in their midst, what rudeness can there be among them?”

SCT p. 425; Analects 9.14, quoted by Mouzi. October 13 reader

CON · The questioner cites Confucius

“The barbarians with a ruler are not so good as the Chinese without one.”

SCT p. 425; Analects 3.5, quoted by the questioner. October 13 reader

Mouzi invokes Confucius’s proposed residence among the “nine barbarian nations.” This supports living among outsiders; it does not itself say that all customs are equally good. The CON quotation follows the assigned translation of a disputed Analects passage.

Learning from foreign teachings

PRO · Mouzi

“What accords with rightness is to be followed, what heals the sick is good. The gentleman-scholar draws widely on all forms of good and thereby benefits his character.”

SCT pp. 421–422. October 13 reader

CON · The questioner cites Mencius

“I have heard of using what is Chinese to change what is barbarian, but I have never heard of using what is barbarian to change what is Chinese.”

SCT p. 425; Mengzi 3A4, quoted by the questioner. October 13 reader

Adopting foreign dress · earlier reading

PRO · The king of Zhao

“Clothes exist to be useful and manners respond to conditions.”

“The King of Zhao Convinces His Uncle to Wear Barbarian Dress,” Ebrey & Walthall, p. 25; trans. J. I. Crump, modified.

CON · Gongzi Cheng, the king’s uncle

“But now the king would discard all this and wear the habit of foreign regions.”

Same passage, Ebrey & Walthall, p. 25.

The king connects Hu dress with mounted archery and border defense. His uncle initially treats the change as abandoning inherited civilization, then accepts the king’s argument.

Huiyuan: monks and royal authority

Huiyuan, A Monk Does Not Bow Down Before a King · SCT pp. 427–429

Huiyuan 慧遠 · Huìyuǎn

334–416 · Mount Lu, southern China

A monk and community leader at Donglin Temple. His A Monk Does Not Bow Down Before a King dates to 404, during the controversy over Huan Xuan’s demand for monastic obeisance.

His distinction: householders follow ordinary family and political duties; monastics serve a religious purpose that warrants a different ritual status.

Biographical background · SCT pp. 427–429.

Those who stay in the household

“Therefore they who rejoice in the way of Śākya invariably first serve their parents and respect their lords.”

SCT p. 427. Oct 13 reader · p. 4

Those who leave it

“Kings and princes, though they have the power of preserving existence, cannot cause a preserved creature to be without woe. ... This is why the monk refuses homage to the Lord of the Myriad Chariots”

SCT pp. 428–429. Oct 13 reader · p. 4

Silla: conversion and royal patronage

Pŏphŭng · Beopheung

The king associated with Silla’s official acceptance of Buddhism. The reading calls him Pŏpkong, his monastic name.

Ich’adon

The court official whose execution and miraculous death mark the conversion account, dated 527.

A later sacred biography

The account survives in Kakhun’s Lives of Eminent Korean Monks (1215). Miracles are the narrative’s claims.

Wŏn’gwang and the petition for troops

걸사표 乞師表

Geolsapyo · Petition Requesting Troops

Requested by: King Chinp’yŏng of Silla.
Addressed to: the Sui court.
Purpose: military help against Koguryŏ.

The petition’s original wording does not survive. The passage below records the monk’s reply to his king, not the text sent to Sui.

Laozi and Wŏn’gwang: killing in war

Laozi, chapter 31 · Wŏn’gwang’s reply and Five Commandments for Laymen

Using force under constraint

Laozi · Weapons and necessity

“Weapons are inauspicious instruments, not the instruments of a cultivated person. But if given no other choice, the cultivated person will use them.”

Laozi 31, trans. Philip J. Ivanhoe, Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy, 3rd ed., p. 187.

Wŏn’gwang’s reported reply to the king

“To destroy others in order to preserve oneself is not the way of a monk. But since I, a poor monk, live in Your Majesty’s territory and waste Your Majesty’s clothes and food, I dare not disobey.”

SKT p. 45. Oct 13 reader · p. 7

Laozi allows the use of weapons when there is no other choice. Wŏn’gwang says destruction violates a monk’s way, but agrees to serve the king who sustains him.

Victory, mourning, and battlefield duty

Laozi · Victory calls for mourning

“A military victory is not a thing of beauty. To beautify victory is to delight in the slaughter of human beings. ... When great numbers of people have been killed, one weeps for them in grief and sorrow. Military victory is associated with the rites of mourning.”

Laozi 31, trans. Philip J. Ivanhoe, p. 187.

Five Commandments for Laymen

“serve your sovereign with loyalty; attend your parents with filial piety; treat your friends with sincerity; do not retreat from a battlefield; be discriminating about the taking of life.”

SKT pp. 45–46. Oct 13 reader · p. 7

Even victory does not justify delight in killing. Laozi prescribes grief and mourning. Wŏn’gwang’s lay precepts combine battlefield loyalty with restraint in taking life; they do not prescribe withdrawal from war.

The first Laozi excerpt restores lines from the full assigned source that the compact reading omitted. The English quotations above retain Ivanhoe’s translation.

Seasonal killing rules before Buddhism in China

Earlier Chinese parallels · Wŏn’gwang’s rules for laypeople

Seasonal restrictions on killing already appear in Warring States Chinese texts. They precede Buddhism’s establishment in China. Wŏn’gwang’s rule combines a spring–summer restriction with Buddhist fasting days.

Earlier Chinese ritual rule · First month of spring

“Nests should not be thrown down; unformed insects should not be killed, nor creatures in the Womb, nor very young creatures”

Liji (Book of Rites), “Yue Ling” (Monthly Ordinances), §10; trans. James Legge. Read the source

Wŏn’gwang · Choosing the time

“Not to kill during the months of spring and summer nor during the six meatless feast days is to choose the time.”

SKT p. 46. October 13 reader · p. 7

Xunzi · 3rd century BCE

Wangzhi (“The Regulations of a King”), chapter 9, bars nets and poisons from marshes while aquatic animals breed. It explicitly protects their growth and links seasonal restraint to plentiful resources.

Primary text · §22 · summary, not a quotation.

Lüshi chunqiu · c. 239 BCE

The first spring month’s ordinances prohibit destroying nests and killing unborn or young creatures. Fawns and eggs are protected too. Closely parallel wording appears in the Liji.

Text and parallel passages · Compilation date. Summary.

Wŏn’gwang · Early 7th century CE

The reported teaching adds six Buddhist observance days to the seasonal rule. It also selects which creatures may be killed and discourages killing often.

SKT p. 46 · Samguk yusa translation, pp. 473–474, note 22. Summary.

The distinction: earlier Chinese rules protect reproduction and regulate taking animal life by season; Buddhist precepts supply a broader objection to killing. The resemblance is clear. Direct borrowing by Wŏn’gwang from a particular Chinese text is not established by these passages.

“Birthing season” is an explanation of the seasonal logic, not Wŏn’gwang’s wording in the assigned translation. His text says “spring and summer.” The account is preserved in later compilations, not a surviving contemporary transcript.

Wang Xizhi: handwriting and printed type

Wang Xizhi 王羲之 · Wáng Xīzhī · fourth-century Eastern Jin · Ebrey pp. 64, 70

An aristocrat, official, and celebrated calligrapher. Ebrey describes copying his work as a way to train the hand. The example here is a later modelbook reproduction of Timely Clearing After Snowfall, not Wang’s original sheet.

The same four characters in print

快雪時晴 · Kuài xuě shí qíng
“Clearing after sudden snow.”

Select a character to locate it in the model.

The model uses running script (xíngshū): strokes vary and movement links their forms. The printed characters use modern sans-serif type.

Five-minute calligraphy comparison

  1. Copy the four printed characters once.
  2. Copy their forms from the historical model.
  3. Circle differences in spacing, proportion, and connections.

Pen or pencil is enough for this comparison. A brush produces different changes in line width.
