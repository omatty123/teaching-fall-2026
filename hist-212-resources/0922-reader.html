The Covenant of Tang湯誓

About this text

Palmer’s own list of the earliest texts (Introduction, p. 54) includes this chapter, and he calls it “an early chapter” (p. 30). Chinese title 湯誓, “the Speech of Tang.” It belongs to the twenty-eight chapters Fu Sheng saved after the Qin book ban. “Early” does not mean a transcript: Palmer’s general point (pp. 49–50) is that the book’s speeches were composed after the events, not taken down as they were given.

King Tang said, ‘Gather to me all my people and listen carefully to what I have to say. It is not me, ordinary little me, who has the audacity to rise up here. But it is Heaven that has decreed that the ruler of Xia must be executed, because of all his many crimes.
Person

King Tang 湯

Founder of the Shang dynasty in the tradition; Palmer calls him Tang the Conqueror. Palmer gives the traditional date of his conquest of Xia as 1766 BC.

Term

A formula of self-abasement

Rulers in these texts call themselves small before Heaven, even as they speak in its name.

Term

The Mandate of Heaven (tianming 天命)

The idea that Heaven authorizes a ruler and can withdraw that authority. Palmer calls this chapter the place where “the right of the people to rebel against a corrupt ruler” is first spelled out (p. 30). The same term, tianming, appears in Analects 2.4.

‘Now, you warriors, you are telling me, “Our leader has no thought for us and our needs. He summons us from our work and he does this just to punish Xia.” I hear what you say, but the ruler of Xia has sinned and because I am in awe of the Supreme Ruler, I have no option but to undertake this task.
Term

Shangdi 上帝, Di 帝

The high god of the Shang oracle bones. Palmer renders it several ways: Supreme Ruler, Supreme Heavenly Sovereign Spirit, Emperor of Heaven. The texts use both Di and Tian (Heaven).

‘But I hear you asking, “Of what significance is Xia to us?” Well, the King of Xia has brutally oppressed his people and drained their energy. And the people, why, they have lost all hope and they are crying out, begging, “Is it not time for the sun to die so that we can die also?” So you can see that the ruler of Xia’s lack of virtue leaves me with no option but to go ahead and do this.
Image or echo

The sun

Traditional commentary reads “the sun” as the ruler of Xia himself: the people would perish with him rather than endure him.

Term

de 德

“Virtue” or moral power. A ruler’s de is what earns or loses the Mandate.

‘If you, my people, will join me, a simple solitary man, in carrying out Heaven’s decree, then great will be your reward. Do not doubt me, my people. I will not go back on my words. So, if you do not honour my oath and do as I say, my people, I will kill you and all your children. And I will show no mercy whatsoever.’

The Solemn Pledge of Zhonghui仲虺之誥

About this text

Chinese title 仲虺之誥, “the Announcement of Zhonghui.” Palmer’s list (Introduction, p. 54) puts this chapter among the “Later Texts” that “may have been recomposed in the third to fourth centuries AD,” the chapters long suspected of being Mei Ze’s reworkings (p. 53). He adds that they “probably contain within them shards of earlier texts” (p. 50). Read it as a later account of Shang rhetoric: what did its author want Tang’s minister to say?

Jie 桀

The last ruler of Xia, the model tyrant of the tradition. (“Jei” is Palmer’s spelling.)

‘Come now,’ replied Zhonghui. ‘Heaven gives each generation the passions which can lead to disaster if they’re not guided. However, Heaven also gives each generation a number of people who are wise. And in time, they will come to control these passions and impulses. Let’s be frank, the ruler of Xia lost sight of what is virtuous. As a result, the people were living in a state of great fear. This is why Heaven gave our king the courage and the wisdom so he can show the many states of our land how to behave well. He can be guided by the ancient ways of Yu the Great, and therefore he can be worthy of the Mandate of Heaven.

Zhonghui 仲虺

Tang’s advisor and the key speaker of chapter 11. Palmer notes he justifies the rebellion by citing the Mandate of Heaven (p. 30).

Person

Yu 禹

Founder of the Xia dynasty and controller of the flood. Palmer gives the traditional dates of Xia as 2205 to 1766 BC. Today’s artwork is the Da Yu ding.

tianming 天命

Here the Shang hold it. The Zhou later claim it (Ebrey pp. 15–19).

‘The King of Xia caused offence by his false claims that he has the blessing of the Supreme Heaven and that, as a result, he has the right to decide what the people should and shouldn’t do. This is not how an emperor should behave and this is why the Shang have been given the Mandate of Heaven – because of our love of justice, and so that we can become the teachers of all the people.
Term

“Emperor” is the translator’s word

The Chinese word is di 帝. No one holds the title of emperor (huangdi) until Qin, in 221 BCE (Oct 6).

‘In order to try and seem to be more in control, the Xia not only ignored the wise people but also ridiculed them – while busily promoting and praising the powerful. This is why we Shang were at first of no significance to the ruler of Xia. We were like weeds in a field of ripe corn. Our people, great or small, lived in constant fear of persecution, even though they hadn’t done anything wrong. Our leader, being a virtuous man, could not help but speak out against it and you can imagine how this led to his actions being publicly criticized by others.
‘So our king did not go near wild, licentious parties. Nor was he interested in personal wealth or prosperity. Instead, he gave offices to people who were really virtuous, appropriate to their integrity. And he gave great rewards to people who strove to do good deeds. He knew that if he treated others as he would treat himself; if he corrected himself, as he would correct others who do wrong; if he was truly just and benevolent … Why, then the people would trust him.
‘Do you remember how the ruler of Ge attacked those innocent people who were generously bringing materials for the sacrifice? And Tang had to take matters into his own hands? Yet when he restored order in the east, the barbarians in the west complained. When he did the same in the south, those in the north complained, asking, “Why does he ignore us?” No matter where he went, people rejoiced, saying, “We have longed for so long for our prince to come and now that he has, all is well again.” This is why the people’s support and admiration for the Shang is so well established.

Ge 葛

A small state Tang punishes first, in the tradition, for attacking the people bringing sacrificial offerings. Retold in Mengzi 3B5 (Sep 29).

judge who is capable and work with the virtuous;
pick out who’s loyal and encourage the good;
support the weak and square up to the stubborn;
challenge the rebels and chasten the dissolute.
If you do this, whatever is failing will simply collapse

The Declaration of Tang湯誥

About this text

Chinese title 湯誥, “the Announcement of Tang.” Palmer’s list (Introduction, p. 54) places this chapter with the “Later Texts” as well: possibly early material, possibly recomposed in the third to fourth centuries AD. Ask what changes if the Mandate speech and the “moral sense” line were composed centuries after Tang.

When King Tang came home after the conquest of the Xia, he came to Bo and there – before all the people – he made this declaration.
Place

Bo 亳

Tang’s capital in the tradition.

‘My people,’ said the king. ‘All of you, from every region of the land, listen carefully to what I (a straightforward man) am about to say. The Emperor above all Emperors has endowed every person with a moral sense, and this is their essential, original nature. However, to ensure that they stay true to this essential nature, it is necessary to have rulers.
Term

Shangdi again

Shangdi 上帝, the high god. “Emperor” is Palmer’s word; no emperor exists before 221 BCE.

Term

zhong 衷 and hengxing 恒性

The Chinese speaks of Heaven sending down zhong 衷, an inner moral core, and a constant nature (hengxing 恒性). Palmer renders it “a moral sense” and “essential, original nature.”

‘The King of Xia lost sight of virtue and became an oppressive dictator. He even oppressed you, my dear people from every region. And when you were no longer able to bear his bitter and poisonous ways, wrought through his cruel regime, you united to proclaim your innocence before everything in Heaven and everything on Earth. You know it is the Way of Heaven to bring good fortune to the good and to curse the wicked. This is why Heaven has brought disaster upon the Xia, making their terrible sins apparent to everyone.
‘This is why I, unimportant as I am, like a child
have been granted the Mandate of Heaven
which lights up the whole world with its authority.
I cannot ignore it or stop doing what is asked of me.
To prove this, I will now offer this dark-skinned sacrifice
to highlight the Supreme Heavenly Sovereign Spirit
and the depths of the crimes of the Xia!
I have asked the Supreme Heavenly Sovereign Spirit to join me
combining our strengths, and to request from Heaven
its authority to be granted to all of you men
so that together we can vanquish the oppressor! Be assured.
The Mandate of Heaven cannot fail.
You, my people, are like the buds and the flowers –
you have come forth. This is my cause, my reason.
I must resolve this for once and for all
simple man that I am, so that all will be well
for you – and also your families.

Part 2 · Kongzi, the Analects

Ivanhoe & Van Norden, Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy, 3rd ed., trans. Edward Slingerland. The 34 passages, with the packet’s own notes beside them.

Kongzi (551–479 BCE) is not proposing a new order. He believes the right order already existed, under the early Zhou kings four centuries before him, and that what held it together was li 禮, the rites: sacrifices, mourning, court protocol, how you sit, how you speak to your father. In his own lifetime the Zhou kings are figureheads and real power belongs to local strongmen who observe the rites when convenient. Kongzi's claim is that these two facts are one fact.

The Analects is not an argument, it is a record of a teacher talking. It does not build. So read it in the six clusters below, and as you go, keep a running answer to one question: what exactly does Kongzi think holds a state together, if it is not law and not force?

Four words to keep straight. Ren 仁, Goodness or humaneness. Li 禮, ritual. Junzi 君子, the gentleman, a moral rank Kongzi detaches from birth. De 德, Virtue, closer to a charisma or a power than to our "virtue."

Packet note

V · The family, and the hard case

Packet note

Do not resolve 13.18 quickly. Kongzi is told about a man who obeyed the law against his own father, and he says that man is not upright. Either this defends the thing that holds a society together beneath the law, or it licenses corruption in the language of virtue. Both readings are 2,500 years old. And notice what Kongzi does not do in 17.21: he never says the rite is commanded. He asks Zai Wo how he would feel. The rite is grounded in a debt, and the argument only works on someone who can still feel it.

VI · Heaven, the spirits, and human nature

Packet note

King Wen 文王

Founder-figure of the Zhou, the dynasty that followed the Shang (Ebrey pp. 15–19). Wen 文 also means “culture.”

11.12 · p.31Zilu asked about serving ghosts and spirits. The Master said, "You are not yet able to serve people — how could you be able to serve ghosts and spirits?" "May I inquire about death?" "You do not yet understand life — how could you possibly understand death?"
17.2 · p.48The Master said, "By nature people are similar; they diverge as the result of practice."
Claim

Eight words on human nature

The one time he speaks about nature, he does not say whether it is good or bad.

How the passages connect

1 · Shame. Start with 2.3. Kongzi says punishment makes people dodge the rules, and ritual makes them ashamed to break them. If that is wrong, his whole idea of government falls apart. So where does the shame come from? 12.1 says it comes from restraining yourself, not from a rule someone hands you. 3.3 says ritual without Ren cannot produce it. 12.19 says the same about a whole state: the ruler is the wind and the people are the grass. Follow that one thread and you have the Ru position.

2 · The family. It is where you learn to be good, and it is where things go wrong. 1.2 makes filial piety the root of Goodness. 2.21 says being a good son already counts as governing. 17.21 says the long mourning repays what you owe your parents. Then comes 13.18, where family and law collide. The same belief gives you the system and its worst problem, so 13.18 cannot be set aside as a strange one-off.

3 · Names. A name comes with duties. A gu that is not a real gu (6.25), a lord who is not a real lord (12.11), and the rectification of names (13.3) are one idea used on a cup, a household, and a state. Follow it forward to Mengzi 1B8, where a king who has stopped being a king can be killed and it does not count as regicide.

4 · He says he invented nothing, and he changes a lot. In 7.1 he passes things on and does not make them up. 15.39 opens teaching to everyone. 2.4 and 4.16 make junzi something you become by learning, not by birth. Put those together and a program that looks backward ends up undercutting the aristocracy. Say so in the debate before Fa does.

5 · What he leaves unsaid. In 5.13 a disciple complains that he will not talk about human nature. In 17.2 he does, once, in eight words that will not say good or bad. In 11.12 and 6.22 the spirits are put off, not denied. Mengzi, Xunzi, Mozi, and the Buddhists each take up one of those gaps.

Three questions to bring to class

1. 2.3. Why should ritual produce shame when punishment produces only evasion? What is shame doing that fear cannot do? Answer this and you have the Ru position; fail to and Han Feizi has already won.

2. 13.18 next to 13.3. In 13.3 a state falls apart when words stop matching things. In 13.18 a father is called upright for covering up a theft. Are these the same Kongzi?

3. 12.7. He ranks the people's confidence above their food. Is that a moral claim or a practical one, and would Kongzi accept the distinction?
