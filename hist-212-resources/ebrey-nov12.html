Japan’s Middle Ages

Chapter 13 · Questions on p. 212

What impact did they have on the center?

“They” are people outside the ruling circle in Kyoto. The “center” is the imperial court and the shogun’s government in that city. The people in Kyoto who gave orders depended on people elsewhere to carry them out. The shogun appointed military governors (shugo), but a governor as far away as Kyushu could bargain for a free hand at home in return for his support.

Even people without an office could change the terms. Between 1428 and 1526, local leagues rose up 24 times in and around Kyoto to demand that moneylenders cancel debts. Many won at least part of what they demanded. pp. 213, 221–222

How did men and women protect their interests?

A warrior could choose which imperial court to back while two rival lines claimed the throne. A samurai wife managed the household and sent supplies when her husband went to war. Some women in farming households continued to trade land and manage their own property after marriage.

Hino Meishi’s family put this into practice. After her husband was killed, his mother hid the birth of Meishi’s son from Go-Daigo’s court. When the rival court returned to power, Meishi used her own ties there to advance the boy. Two women protected the same child, first by hiding him and then by getting him recognized. pp. 213–215

How did the arts manage to flourish in the midst of turmoil?

Patrons still paid for performances and poems, and artists could travel to find them. Shoguns and provincial lords performed Noh alongside professional actors. In linked verse (renga), poets took turns adding lines. A skilled poet of humble birth could earn a place in the gathering. The monk Sōchō traveled between patrons and received a poetry commission from the shogun’s deputy during the civil wars. pp. 217–218, 220–221

China under Mongol rule

Chapter 12 · Questions on p. 198

How did Jin and Song officials organize resistance, and why did it fail?

Jin moved its court south to Kaifeng and tried to hold the remaining territory. Assassinations divided its leadership, and the Mongols recruited soldiers and siege specialists from the people they conquered. Song raised money, fielded a navy and defended the river fortress of Xiangyang for five years. Its fall in 1273 opened the route toward the Yangzi. Commanders who had joined the Mongols then persuaded former comrades to surrender. pp. 198–201

What policies did the Mongols put in place?

Yuan rulers registered households by occupation and ranked the population in four groups. The categories affected taxes, legal treatment and access to office. A northern household could be taxed as a family while a southern household paid by acreage. Even after civil service examinations resumed, quotas limited who could advance. pp. 202–203, 206

Why did the Yuan Dynasty in China fail to last even two centuries?

Court factions fought over the succession, and inflation eroded the value of paper money. Cold weather and disease made recovery harder. As the court lost the ability to keep order, people relied on whoever could organize a local defense. By the time Yuan rule in China ended in 1368, much of that power had already passed out of the court’s hands. pp. 205–206, 209

Did the experience of bitter defeat have any long-term effects on Chinese culture?

Yes. Some painters used older styles to express loyalty to the world they had lost. Zhao Mengfu took office under the Yuan and also belonged to a circle that painted this way. The narrowing of careers in government had another cultural effect. Educated writers found work with theater companies, helping turn Yuan drama into a major literary form. pp. 206–209

What was going on in society at local levels, beyond the purview of Mongol rulers?

Educated families still had land, skills and neighbors who needed them. Some organized relatives into lineages, founded local academies or supported charity. Others earned a living teaching children, practicing medicine or selling books. Losing a route to office changed where these people worked and who benefited from their education. pp. 207–209

Kyoto, Kamakura and Yoshino

1333 · Kamakura falls
Emperor Go-Daigo wants to rule directly. Ashikaga Takauji, a warrior sent against him, switches sides and helps him regain power.
1338 · A shogun and two courts
Takauji breaks with Go-Daigo, backs a rival emperor in Kyoto and becomes shogun, the military ruler. Muromachi is the Kyoto neighborhood where he governs. Go-Daigo’s line holds a rival court to the south at Yoshino.
1392 · The courts reunite
Takauji’s grandson Yoshimitsu brokers a settlement. The throne is reunited, but the shogun still needs provincial military governors to govern beyond Kyoto. pp. 212–213
1467–1477 · Ōnin War
Rival warrior houses fight in Kyoto. As the fighting shifts to the provinces, local warriors and leagues gain room to act. pp. 219–222
