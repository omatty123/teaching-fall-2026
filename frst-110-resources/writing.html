8Hedge the link, not the fact.“Ma laoshi, you say hedge, then you say don’t hedge!” Both. Here’s when.

Hedge the link"Show" claims a direct connection with no gap, one nobody could see any other way. Putting two works together is your move, so "suggest" or "seem to show."
When these sources are read together, they show how the United States government takes priority of one social class over the other…
→
Read together, these sources suggest that the government puts one social class ahead of another.
Don't hedge the factWhen the text or the record says it plainly, say it plainly. "Probably," "may have," and "perhaps" make you sound unsure of what you know.
The United States government received heavy backlash after the events of Hurricane Katrina, probably because of the low effort recovery plan…
→
The government took heavy backlash after Katrina because its recovery effort left people like Ethel to die.

9Bring your reader along.Your reader is a companion who wants a good story while they learn something. Walk them through it.

Make a claim, then turn it

“Patricia Smith’s Blood Dazzler is an angry book. … This anger is grief. It burns and burns from a well of anguish, forcing the reader to feel its rage and sadness.”

A blunt claim, then four words that turn it: "This anger is grief." We feel the turn with her.

Take the second look together

“At first glance, this passage is only about wealthy individuals ignoring a lower-class man with a following mostly of poor people, but it also touches on the complex relationship between humans and the divine.”

"At first glance" admits what we'd all see first, then leads us somewhere deeper.

Transitions

10Make transitions build.A transition tells us how the next point grows out of the last one. Don't just add the next item.

The grocery listTransitions that only add the next item: "Another," "Also," "On the other hand."
On the other hand, Blood Dazzler portrays the harm…
→
Egan's harm came from ignorance. Smith's came from people who knew better.
Build on the last pointSay how the new example changes what we know.
Another time when abandonment occurs in Blood Dazzler is in the poem 34.
→
Worse still, in “34” abandonment turns deadly: thirty-four people drowned in a nursing home no one evacuated (Smith 50).
Raise the stakes

“Even worse may have been the human response to these invasions.”

Two words, "Even worse," and we know the next paragraph will hit harder than the last.

Conclusion

11Take your thesis seriously.If it's true, something follows. Say what. Don't escape into "if only," a question, or a cliché.

The "if only""If only they knew." "If only they cared more." Our works show that is never enough.
THEY KNEWEgan: "The U.S. and Canadian governments did nothing with the information" (113).
Smith: Brown got the report and asked, "Anything specific I need to do / or tweak?" (28)
→
So don't stop at knowing or caring. Say what it takes to act, and keep acting, or why people fail even when they care.
The question exitEnding on a question instead of an answer.
Seeing how these injustices have still been shown in recent years, when will the government truly step in?
→
If the system hasn't changed, the next Katrina will drown the same neighborhoods.
The safe clichéEnding on something nice that no one could disagree with.
Therefore, any action we take must be well-informed and cautious of potential ramifications.
→
Information fixes ignorance. Neglect needs someone held to account.

12Bring it into the present.Your works happened then. Your reader lives now. Tell us what they mean for today.

Switch to the present tense

“The United States seems to believe, that only individuals that are of value to the nation deserve to be saved, when in reality, all those that call the United States home should be saved by the government that swore to protect them.”

Katrina was 2005, but "seems to believe" is now. The paper ends in our country, not theirs.

13Use verbs that do something.“Show,” “focus on,” “showcase the theme of”: these verbs say nothing. Name the action.

Charley Patton’s song and Patricia Smiths collection of poems both showcase the theme of systemic neglect.

“Suggest” is for the link you draw between two works (#8). Everywhere else, name what the writer does.

“This anger is grief. It burns and burns from a well of anguish, forcing the reader to feel its rage and sadness.”

14Say who did what to whom.“Stuck,” “abandoned,” “they”: someone did this. Put them in the sentence.

In this song Charley Patton is singing about going to a desirable place while being stuck in the floods.

He sings “they got me barred.” Someone barred him. Say who.

STRONGER

“In the middle of a disaster, the workers had to make the decision to leave without the elders, throwing all of their lives at Katrina’s lethal hands.”

15Cut the filler.Two habits that keep a smart writer sounding ordinary.

Throat-clearingA whole sentence that warms up instead of making the point.
While both storms are tragic, without a doubt, and the basis of both destructive elements is the water, there is still a key similarity. Faith…
→
Both stories run on faith.
The stickerSlapping "interesting," "important," or "notable" on something instead of saying why. Keep the move, swap the sticker for the reason.
A notable case study in The Death and Life of the Great Lakes is the St. Lawrence Seaway…
→
The St. Lawrence Seaway shows what uninformed action costs: its locks were obsolete "almost before the freshly poured concrete could dry" (Egan 24).

16Don't repeat yourself.Same words, same sentence shape, same length, same tone: your reader tunes out.

ONE PAPER, THREE TIMES
  1. 1Although Egan focuses on environmental damage and Smith emphasizes human suffering, both portray water as more than a passive element of nature.
  2. 2Although Egan emphasizes ecological consequences and Smith portrays human suffering, both writers use water to expose realities people often overlook.
  3. 3Although Egan focuses on ecological damage while Smith emphasizes human loss, both works portray water as a reminder of destruction and its lasting effects.
→
SAME WORDS, NEW ORDER
  1. 1Although Egan focuses on environmental damage and Smith emphasizes human suffering, both portray water as more than a passive element of nature.
  2. 2Both writers use water to expose realities people often overlook: ecological consequences for Egan, human suffering for Smith.
  3. 3Water is a reminder of destruction and its lasting effects, whether the damage is ecological, as in Egan, or human, as in Smith.

17No comma splices.Two complete sentences joined by a comma make a comma splice. Use a period or a semicolon, or add a joining word.

He is no stranger to abandonment, his fellow FEMA employees are watching from afar while he is trying to work on site.
→
He is no stranger to abandonment. His fellow FEMA employees are watching from afar while he is trying to work on site.
