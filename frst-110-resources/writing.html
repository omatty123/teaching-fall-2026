Don't bury the lede.The lede is your main point. Don't scatter it. Gather it up front.

Frame the start.When your paper begins with a person or a place, one sentence can bring us in before your first claim.
NO FRAME“Patricia Smith, through the medium of poetry, writes about the many displaced and abandoned citizens of New Orleans…”
→
ONE FRAME SENTENCEIn the days after Katrina, Ethel Freeman waited in her wheelchair outside the New Orleans Convention Center for help that never came. Patricia Smith, through the medium of poetry, writes about the many displaced and abandoned citizens of New Orleans…

We meet a person before we meet an argument.

Give your title a second half.A one-word title names the topic. A second half names your argument.

Body

Prove it, with both works read fairly and every claim backed up.

Let the two works differ.The differences are where the synthesis happens. Don’t bend one work until it matches the other.

Bent to match
Both works provide direct, concise orders to evacuate, but the speakers are still met with hesitation and disregard.
→
Noah’s people could act and refused. Smith’s people heard the warning and had no way out: “Like our bodies got wheels and gas” (Smith 7).
The difference is the argument

“Through personification, Egan presents water as a witness to human history and a graveyard for its consequences, while Smith depicts it as an unpredictable force that exposes human vulnerability.”

Witness against force. Now there’s something to argue about.

Use more of your evidence.One poem against one song is thin. The rest of the book often makes your point for you.
For example, President Bush flew over the aftermath and floods, in the presidential aircraft while he could have been in office working on these issues instead of just viewing the horrors.
→
Smith puts us in his seat. In “The President Flies Over,” he says, “I don’t ever have to come down” (Smith 36).

The paper had the point and left the poem that proves it on the shelf.

Name who is speaking.Smith writes in many voices: the storm, the Superdome, the president, people left behind. The poet isn’t the speaker, and one speaker isn’t all of them.
Patricia Smith (“Man on the TV Say”) and Charley Patton (“High Water Everywhere, Part 1”) both cannot leave.

The poet and the singer aren’t stuck; the people in their works are.

STRONGER

“Within the poem ‘Ethel’s Sestina’, Patricia Smith uses the perspective of Ethel in which she writes, ‘I trust his every word. Herbert my son.’”

Smith writes the poem. Ethel speaks it. This writer keeps them apart.

Back up big claims. Don’t overstate.Claims about the world need evidence, and they need limits.
This was something commonly heard among these communities.

Name your source, or cut the claim.

Hurricane Katrina destroyed the city of New Orleans in 2005.
→
By August 31, 2005, about 80 percent of New Orleans was under water.

The number is stronger than the exaggeration. Source: Effects of Hurricane Katrina in New Orleans.

Hedge the link, not the fact.“Ma laoshi, you say hedge, then you say don’t hedge!” Both. Here’s when.
Hedge the link“Show” claims the connection is plain to anyone. Putting two works together is your move, so “suggest” or “seem to show.”
When these sources are read together, they show how the United States government takes priority of one social class over the other…
→
Read together, these sources suggest that the government puts one social class ahead of another.
Don't hedge the factWhen the record says it plainly, say it plainly. “Probably” makes you sound unsure of what you know.
The United States government received heavy backlash after the events of Hurricane Katrina, probably because of the low effort recovery plan…
→
The government took heavy backlash for its slow response. Ethel Freeman died in her wheelchair outside the Convention Center, waiting for help.
Bring your reader along.Your reader is a companion who wants a good story while they learn something. Walk them through it.
Make a claim, then turn it

“Patricia Smith’s Blood Dazzler is an angry book. … This anger is grief. It burns and burns from a well of anguish, forcing the reader to feel its rage and sadness.”

A blunt claim, then four words that turn it: “This anger is grief.” We feel the turn with her.

Take the second look together

“At first glance, this passage is only about wealthy individuals ignoring a lower-class man with a following mostly of poor people, but it also touches on the complex relationship between humans and the divine.”

“At first glance” admits what we’d all see first, then leads us somewhere deeper.

Transitions

Every paragraph builds on the one before it.

Make transitions build.The first sentence of a paragraph tells us how it grows out of the last one.

END OF ONE PARAGRAPH

“Here he is mocking the government and president George W. Bush for creating this narrative that this storm was God’s doing, when in reality the Government is the real one to have abandoned them.”

START OF THE NEXTAnother time when abandonment occurs in Blood Dazzler is in the poem 34.
→
BUILT ON ITIn “34,” the abandonment comes closer. This time it’s the caretakers who leave, and thirty-four people drown (Smith 50).

“Another” adds the next item. “Comes closer” tells us why this one matters more.

Only promise a contrast you deliver.“On the other hand” promises the opposite. If the next point isn’t one, it’s just the next item.
On the other hand, Blood Dazzler portrays the harm…
→
Egan’s harm built over decades. Smith’s came in a single week.
Raise the stakes.Let the reader feel the next point is heavier than the last.

“Even worse may have been the human response to these invasions.”

Two words, “Even worse,” and we know the next paragraph will hit harder.

Conclusion

Say what follows from your argument, and why it matters now.

Take your thesis seriously.If it's true, something follows. Say what. Don't escape into “if only,” a question, or a cliché.

The “if only”“If only they knew.” “If only they cared more.” Our works show that is never enough.
THEY KNEWEgan: “The U.S. and Canadian governments did nothing with the information” (113).
Smith: Brown got the report and asked, “Anything specific I need to do / or tweak?” (28)
→
So don't stop at knowing or caring. Say what it takes to act, and keep acting, or why people fail even when they care.
The question exitEnding on a question instead of an answer.
Seeing how these injustices have still been shown in recent years, when will the government truly step in?
→
If the system hasn't changed, the next Katrina will drown the same neighborhoods.
The safe clichéEnding on something nice that no one could disagree with.
Therefore, any action we take must be well-informed and cautious of potential ramifications.
→
Information fixes ignorance. Neglect needs someone held to account.
Bring it into the present.When your works speak to now, end where we live.
…they still deserve the same treatment that the United States government supposedly promised to everyone.
→
…promised to everyone. Our neighbors are still waiting on that promise. An EF-3 tornado hit Menasha on July 27, 2026; the federal disaster declaration came October 7, ten weeks later.

Katrina was 2005. Menasha was this summer, and the agency is the same: FEMA. Sources: Fox 11, WBAY.

Polish

Every sentence earns its place, and every quotation is exact.

Sentences

Use verbs that do something.“Focus on,” “explore,” “showcase the theme of”: these verbs say nothing. Name the action.

Charley Patton’s song and Patricia Smiths collection of poems both showcase the theme of systemic neglect.
→
Patton’s song and Smith’s poems both put that neglect on trial.

“Suggest” is for the link you draw between two works (see Hedge the link, in Body). Everywhere else, name what the writer does.

Say who did what to whom.“Stuck,” “abandoned,” “they”: someone did this. Put them in the sentence.
In this song Charley Patton is singing about going to a desirable place while being stuck in the floods.

He sings “they got me barred.” Someone barred him. Say who.

STRONGER

“In the middle of a disaster, the workers had to make the decision to leave without the elders, throwing all of their lives at Katrina’s lethal hands.”

Cut the filler.Two habits that keep a smart writer sounding ordinary.
The warm-up sentenceA whole sentence that gets ready instead of making the point.
While both storms are tragic, without a doubt, and the basis of both destructive elements is the water, there is still a key similarity. Faith…
→
Both stories run on faith.
The empty label“Interesting,” “important,” or “notable” in place of the reason. Give the reason.
A notable case study in The Death and Life of the Great Lakes is the St. Lawrence Seaway…
→
Uninformed action built the St. Lawrence Seaway: its locks were obsolete “almost before the freshly poured concrete could dry” (Egan 24).
Don't repeat yourself.Same words, same sentence shape, same length, same tone: your reader tunes out. Say it once, then build on it.
ONE PAPER, THREE TIMES
  1. 1Although Egan focuses on environmental damage and Smith emphasizes human suffering, both portray water as more than a passive element of nature.
  2. 2Although Egan emphasizes ecological consequences and Smith portrays human suffering, both writers use water to expose realities people often overlook.
  3. 3Although Egan focuses on ecological damage while Smith emphasizes human loss, both works portray water as a reminder of destruction and its lasting effects.
→
SAID ONCE, THEN BUILT ON
  1. 1Although Egan focuses on environmental damage and Smith emphasizes human suffering, both portray water as more than a passive element of nature.
  2. 2In Egan, water carries the invaders people let in.
  3. 3In Smith, water speaks for itself: Katrina tells her own story.
No comma splices.Two complete sentences joined by a comma make a comma splice. Use a period or a semicolon, or add a joining word.
He is no stranger to abandonment, his fellow FEMA employees are watching from afar while he is trying to work on site.
→
He is no stranger to abandonment. His fellow FEMA employees are watching from afar while he is trying to work on site.

Quotations

Quote exactly.One extra word changes the line.

THE PAPER“makes you drink the rain”
→
SMITH, P. VIII“makes you drink rain”

“The rain” is the storm we know. “Rain” is any rain, all of it.

Commas and periods go inside.In American English, a comma or period that ends a quotation goes inside the quotation marks.
Inside the marks
If “thy will” was done “on earth as it is in Heaven”, then those people would have been saved…
→
If “thy will” was done “on earth as it is in Heaven,” then those people would have been saved…
After the citationWhen a page number follows the quotation, the period waits until after the parentheses.

The elders “gaze upon the preacher with unquestioned reverence” (Smith 17).
