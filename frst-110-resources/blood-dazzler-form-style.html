“7 p.m., Thursday, August 25, 2005” · p. 5

Polysyndeton is the repeated use of conjunctions such as “and.” Smith gives Katrina a first-person voice and joins its sensations through repeated “and.”

I see
what this language does
and taste
soil on my tongue
and feel
brick splintering spine

Opening excerpt, p. 5. Line breaks retained; indentation approximated; bold added. The poem continues through hearing and ends with wanting “all.”

Several devices operate together
PolysyndetonThe repeated “and” adds one sensation to another. It can make Katrina’s appetite sound insistent and unending.
AnaphoraRepetition at the beginnings of successive units. Here, “and” also begins successive phrases. The same words can perform both functions.
ParallelismSimilar grammatical structures: seeing, tasting, feeling, hearing, wanting. Their repeated structure makes the final shift from perception to desire noticeable.
Line breaks and spacingShort phrases alternate with indented continuations. Reading across those breaks separates the act of sensing from what Katrina senses.
PersonificationThe storm speaks and desires as if it were a person. Its “I” is Katrina’s constructed voice, not automatically the poet’s own voice.
SoundThe repeated sp sound in “splintering spine” is alliteration. The violent image and concentrated consonants can make the phrase sound forceful.

A possible reading: the repeated structure makes destruction sound like a sequence of experiences Katrina wants to collect. The factual landfall notice above the poem and the storm’s hungry voice give us two different ways of representing the same event.

“What Was the First Sound” · pp. 19–20

Anadiplosis repeats an ending word or phrase at the beginning of the next unit. Here it links stanzas. “Chained verses” describes that structure.

One stanza’s ending becomes the next stanza’s beginning
EndingNext beginning
“under August drape.”“Under August drape,”
“considered bodies.”“Consider bodies,”
“Wind found its color.”“Wind found its color”

The repetition also changes meaning. “Considered bodies” describes Katrina’s gaze; “Consider bodies” can address us as readers. The connected stanzas move through bodies, buildings, trees, wind, and rain.

Synesthesia combines different senses. “The sound was purple” gives sound a color. It can make the storm feel difficult to contain within an ordinary description; a reader might also find the combination strained.

Tanka and sestina: larger patterns

Form is the poem’s arrangement; style includes its characteristic choices of language, sound, and voice. They overlap: repetition can shape both an individual phrase and an entire poem.

“Tankas”
pp. 38–39		Five-line units give brief experiences a shared form. In the child-loss passage, “is—was” changes tense within one line: the speaker’s recognition of death happens inside the pattern.
“Ethel’s Sestina”
pp. 45–46		Six words return in changing order. They keep returning us to waiting. Smith then expands the pattern with separate repetitions of “Come.” Repetition can enact both confinement and an imagined departure.

Why might it sound “cringe”?

“Cringe” names a reaction, but it does not yet explain its cause. A poem’s repetition, intense imagery, or speaking voice may feel awkward, excessive, or too obviously constructed. That response is worth examining; you do not have to decide that every choice succeeds.

  • Repetition may feel overdone. Repeated “and” can sound insistent, but it may also make a reader impatient.
  • Intensity may feel forced. A phrase such as “brick splintering spine” concentrates violence and sound. A reader may feel the poem is pressing too hard for a reaction.
  • A speaking storm makes the invention obvious. Katrina’s human appetite can be disturbing or theatrical. Whether that theatricality works is a question to support with evidence.
  • Reading aloud changes the effect. Stressing every repetition can make a passage sound more emphatic than a quieter reading. Distinguish the words and layout from a particular performance.

Regional speech or an unfamiliar voice is not, by itself, evidence of weak writing. Name the particular choice and what it does.

What analysis can sound like: “The repeated ‘and’ feels excessive to me because each new sensation receives another emphatic beginning. That excess may fit Katrina’s appetite, although I find it makes the voice too theatrical.”

This identifies a feature, explains a response, considers its purpose, and makes a judgment. Recognizing a purpose does not require liking the result.

How the parts make a whole

Poetry is a technology for arranging language: line breaks, repeated words, voices, and forms control what comes together and what remains distinct. Smith places official statements, a speaking storm, and imagined human experiences within one book.

A possible claim: “Together, these poems seem to be working toward a world in which the hurricane is both a physical event and many different lived experiences. Repetition connects those experiences; changes of voice and form preserve their differences.”

A claim about the whole becomes stronger when it names the parts that support it: Katrina’s accumulating “and,” the linked stanzas, the brief tankas, and Ethel’s returning end words.
