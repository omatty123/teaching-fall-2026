Manuscripts, languages, and evidence

How do we know this text?

Genesis reaches us through generations of handwritten copies and translations. We can inspect those copies, compare their wording, and explain how an English translation was made. We do not have an author’s original manuscript.

Did someone dig up Genesis?

Genesis was already being copied, read, and interpreted long before modern archaeology. Unlike the recovery of Akkadian Gilgamesh, its transmission continued in religious communities. Discoveries such as the Dead Sea Scrolls added much earlier copies to the evidence; they did not introduce an unknown book to the world.

Two kinds of surviving evidence

Ancient fragmentsGenesis among the Dead Sea Scrolls

The scroll collection from Qumran, near the Dead Sea east of Jerusalem, dates from roughly the third century BCE to the first century CE. It includes fragmentary copies of Genesis. A fragment preserves only part of a text, so one object cannot supply the whole book.

Inspect the IAA’s “4Q Genesis–Exodus” record ↗ This is a Genesis manuscript witness, not a complete copy of our four chapters.

Early 1000s CEThe Leningrad Codex

A codex is a book with leaves rather than a rolled scroll. This manuscript preserves the complete Hebrew Bible, including Genesis. It belongs to the Masoretic tradition: Jewish scribes carefully transmitted the text along with signs and notes guiding its reading.

It is held at the National Library of Russia in Saint Petersburg. Modern scholarly Hebrew editions use this manuscript as an important base for comparison. Holding library ↗ · Netherlands-Flemish Bible Society: the manuscript ↗

The broad ancient date range describes the Qumran collection, not a single date assigned to every Genesis fragment. Israel Antiquities Authority: contents and dates ↗

How were the older copies found?

Bedouin discoveries near Qumran began in 1947. Further finds by Bedouin searchers and archaeological teams recovered scrolls and fragments from additional caves near the Dead Sea. Scholars had to identify, join, photograph, and publish the pieces.

IAA: discovery and publication ↗

How can a copy be dated?

Scholars compare handwriting with dated examples—a method called paleography—and sometimes use radiocarbon dating of the writing material. A scribe may also leave a dated note. These methods date the surviving copy or material, not the first telling of its story.

IAA: scripts and dating methods ↗

How do marks on a manuscript become English?

  1. Read the writing.

    Genesis is written in Hebrew, using an alphabet read from right to left. Scholars read the letters, including older handwriting styles, and identify damaged or missing portions.

  2. Compare witnesses.

    A “witness” is a surviving copy or version that supplies evidence for the wording. Scholars compare Hebrew manuscripts and ancient translations, including the Greek Septuagint. Copies often agree; some preserve different words or phrases.

  3. Prepare an edition.

    Editors print a Hebrew text with notes recording important alternatives. This comparison of readings is called textual criticism: “criticism” here means examining evidence, not finding fault with a religion.

  4. Translate and explain choices.

    Translators weigh vocabulary, grammar, context, and the manuscript evidence. Your pamphlet’s English text and small footnotes are the result. This webpage transcribes that assigned translation; it is not a new translation from Hebrew.

Does that tell us who wrote Genesis?

Copying history and composition history are different questions. A dated manuscript tells us that a form of the text existed by that time. It does not give us the date of the first draft or the name of its author.

Many scholars infer that the flood account combines earlier traditions by examining repeated episodes, changing vocabulary, and differences such as the animal instructions. Their proposed sources and stages of editing are reconstructions from evidence. We have not excavated separately labeled original drafts that settle the entire process. TheTorah.com: evidence for combined flood traditions ↗

What does the evidence establish?

A manuscript is physical evidence that people copied and transmitted a text. Comparing manuscripts helps establish its wording and history. Whether an event described in the story happened is a further historical question; the existence of an old copy does not settle it by itself.

A missing answer can matter.

“Why destroy everyone?” is a reasonable question raised by the situation. A summary can accurately report that Tablet XI does not state the gods’ initial reason. Saying they had no reason would go beyond that evidence. The difference between giving and withholding an explanation is itself a starting point for analysis.

Questions while reading

What about the confusing parts?

Some questions have a direct answer in the passage. Others expose a tension the text does not resolve.

Did God command vegetarianism?

Earlier, plants are given as food. After the flood, meat is explicitly permitted. Genesis 1:29–30, before our assigned passage, gives plants and fruit to humans and plants to animals. That supplies the background for the reference to green plants in 9:3.

In 9:3, God permits Noah and his sons to eat animals. Verse 4 then forbids eating flesh with its life, identified as its blood. The restriction is on blood, not on all meat. Permission to eat meat is also different from a command requiring everyone to eat it.

Earlier context: Genesis 1:29–30 and its translation notes. The question here concerns what the narrative says, rather than a rule for every later religious community.

What makes an animal “clean” or “unclean”? Does “unclean” mean dirty?

These are religious categories, not descriptions of dirt, disease, or bad behavior. In this story, Noah uses clean animals and birds in an offering to God (8:20). “Ritual” refers here to a religious practice such as making that offering.

A bath would not change the category. For example, later biblical food laws classify a pig as unclean even if it is physically washed (Leviticus 11:7–8). Those detailed food rules are not laid out in Genesis 6–9: the flood story assumes its readers know the distinction, without giving a list of species.

Both kinds are saved. In 7:2, Noah brings seven pairs of clean animals and one pair of animals that are not clean. “Unclean” does not mean “leave it behind to die.” Keep three questions separate: which animals are preserved, which are offered in sacrifice, and what people are permitted to eat.

Two of each animal—or seven pairs? And why sacrifice animals just rescued?

6:19–20 calls for two of each kind. 7:2–3 distinguishes seven pairs of clean animals and birds from one pair of unclean animals. “Clean” is a religious classification, not a description of dirt.

Noah later sacrifices clean animals and birds (8:20); the extra animals help explain how offering some need not eliminate their kind. Scholars also study the differing instructions and repetitions as evidence of combined narrative traditions. Notice the difference before trying to make the wording uniform. Was the flood forty days or 150 days?

7:12 measures forty days of rain; 7:24 describes 150 days of prevailing waters. The account then continues through recession, landing, observation, and drying. Forty days is not the total time inside the ark. Use the timeline to separate these events; do not add every number as a new, consecutive block.

If people are still evil, what did the flood change?

That is a difficulty the wording itself raises. Compare 6:5–7 with 8:21–22: evil in human hearts precedes the destruction and remains afterward. What clearly changes is God’s announced response—a commitment not to repeat the destruction. The passage does not say the flood permanently cured human wrongdoing.

Why curse Canaan when Ham is the one who acts?

The passage does not fully explain the shift. Ham sees his father’s nakedness and tells his brothers (9:22); Noah’s curse names Ham’s son Canaan (9:25). Noah speaks the curse. Keeping the people and speaker straight lets you identify the problem accurately without inventing a missing action by Canaan. See the family diagram.

Does the text’s silence mean something did not happen?

No. Describe the limits of the account. Genesis includes the wives in the ark, but gives them no names or direct speech in this passage. “The wives are not given dialogue” is supported; “the wives never spoke” is not. Likewise, Tablet XI does not state the gods’ original reason for the flood; that does not establish that the gods had no reason.

Ask what the situation leads a reader to expect to learn. Then identify which answers the text supplies, which it leaves open, and whose actions or perspectives it includes. That observation can belong in an accurate summary; explaining its effects begins analysis.

What do the 150 days measure? Genesis 7:24

Forty days measures the rain (7:12); 150 days measures the prevailing waters. Drying and waiting continue afterward. These are not consecutive periods to add together.

Source for this note ↗

Had God forgotten Noah? Genesis 8:1

“Remembered” introduces action on Noah’s behalf: God sends a wind and the water recedes. It need not imply a lapse of memory.

Where is Ararat? Genesis 8:4

Ararat is associated with Urartu, a highland region around Lake Van. “Mountains” names a region here; the verse does not identify today’s Mount Ararat as the exact landing spot.

Source for this note ↗

What is a burnt offering? Genesis 8:20

An animal sacrifice burned on an altar—a place for presenting offerings to a deity.

A connection with Gilgamesh Genesis 8:21

In Gilgamesh XI 161–163, the gods also smell the survivor’s offering. Compare what the gods say and do afterward in each account.

Are humans better after the flood? Genesis 8:21

The human heart is still described as evil. What changes is God’s announced response: the same diagnosis now accompanies a promise not to repeat the destruction.

What is a “reckoning”? Genesis 9:5

Accountability for taking a life. The following verse grounds the protection of human life in humanity’s being made in God’s image.

“My bow in the clouds” Genesis 9:13

The rainbow is the covenant’s sign. In 9:15–16, God is the one who sees it and remembers the promise.

Who is Canaan? Genesis 9:18

Ham’s son and Noah’s grandson. Ham acts in the scene; Canaan is the person Noah later curses.

Why curse Canaan for Ham’s act? Genesis 9:25

The passage does not explain the shift; interpretations differ. Noah speaks the curse. These verses mention no skin color; their later use to justify racial slavery adds that claim.

Survival is not immortality Genesis 9:29

Unlike Uta-napishti in Gilgamesh, Noah survives the flood but eventually dies.

Why “LORD” in capitals? Genesis 6:3

Small-capital LORD represents the Hebrew divine name YHWH. “God” translates a different Hebrew term; both refer to the deity in this story.

Were people supposed to eat only plants? Genesis 9:3

Genesis 1:29–30 gives plants as food. Here, after the flood, eating animals is permitted. The next verse prohibits eating flesh with its blood.
