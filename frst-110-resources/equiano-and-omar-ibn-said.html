Equiano and Omar ibn Said

Two autobiographical works · FRST 110 · October 30, 2026

Both authors write about enslavement and religious life. They write in different languages, for different audiences, and from different positions of freedom.

Olaudah Equiano

The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano, or Gustavus Vassa, the African. Written by Himself.

Published in London after he had purchased his freedom. Its dedication addresses the British Parliament and the slave-trade debate.

Two autobiographical accounts of enslavement

Equiano’s twelve-chapter book and Omar ibn Said’s short Arabic manuscript describe capture, forced Atlantic passage, and life under slavery. Their circumstances of writing, languages, religious traditions, and access to freedom differ.

Equiano’s whole Interesting Narrative and Omar ibn Said’s 1831 autobiography
AspectEquianoOmar ibn Said
Date and form An English book published in 1789, with a dedication to Parliament, a subscriber list, and twelve chapters. It combines a life history with travel, religious testimony, documents, and arguments against the slave trade. Title page, dedication, and contents. An Arabic manuscript written in 1831, containing a brief life account, religious passages, and repeated or revised sections. The surviving handwritten document and its later English translations are distinct sources. Original manuscript; modern Arabic–English edition.
Position when writing Writes as a free man looking back on enslavement. In Chapter VII he reproduces the manumission registered on 11 July 1766 after he paid Robert King £40 sterling. Manumission means an enslaver’s formal release of an enslaved person. Chapter VII. Writes while still enslaved in North Carolina. His account ends with his continued subjection to Jim Owen. Better food, clothing, and treatment do not amount to release from ownership. Final passage, Bird translation.
African life and capture His account places his childhood in Essaka, in Eboe, and describes family life, institutions, and religion before kidnapping at about eleven. His narration presents the Middle Passage through a child’s fear and partial understanding. These are the book’s autobiographical claims. Chapters I–II. Names Futa Toro as his birthplace and describes twenty-five years of study before an army captured him. He says he was thirty-seven when he left Africa. His Atlantic crossing ends in Charleston, where he is sold. Life account, Bird translation.
Language and audience Uses English to address a British reading public and Parliament directly. The title’s “Written by Himself” asserts authorship; the dedication explicitly seeks action on the slave trade. Title and dedication. Addresses “Sheikh Hunter,” his “brothers,” and the people of the Carolinas and America. Arabic connects the writing to his earlier learning, while English-speaking readers depend on translation. These addresses do not establish that he controlled the manuscript’s circulation. Salutations and addresses, Bird translation.
Literacy and learning Describes learning English, asking books to speak, attending school, and seeking instruction from sailors and others. The adult author can now explain experiences the child did not understand. Chapters III–IV. Arrives already educated in Arabic and Islamic learning. He begins by describing difficulty recalling language and writing his life, yet supplies extended religious texts. His account does not make literacy a skill first acquired through enslavement. Opening and account of study, Bird translation.
Religion Presents a Christian life shaped by Providence. Baptism in Chapter IV and the later conversion account in Chapter X are separate events. His Christianity also supplies a standard for condemning Christians who enslave and mistreat others. Chapter X. The notebook contains Qur’an 67 before the life account; the account includes Qur’anic language, Gospel reading, and the Lord’s Prayer. These elements require attention to translation and coercion. They do not justify reducing his religious life to a simple conversion story. Lo and Ernst edition and notes.
Treatment and ownership Distinguishes helpful individuals from cruel ones, but favorable treatment does not secure freedom. Pascal’s sale of him at the end of Chapter IV exposes that vulnerability. Even after manumission, Chapter VIII describes an attempted kidnapping. Chapter IV; Chapter VIII. Condemns Johnson, describes escape and imprisonment, and praises the Owens’ treatment. He firmly refuses a proposed return to Charleston. That refusal expresses a preference among dangerous circumstances; it is not evidence that he freely chose enslavement. Capture, escape, and Owen passages.
Movement and freedom The book continues well beyond his purchase of freedom: maritime work, the Arctic voyage, religious searching, and public action occupy later chapters. Physical travel and legal freedom are related but not identical. Chapters IX–XII. The account moves from West African study to forced passage, Charleston, escape, jail in Fayetteville, and the Owen household in Bladen County. It closes in continued captivity, without a manumission document or a return to Africa. Complete Bird translation.
Political argument and its limits Directly campaigns against the slave trade and argues that British commerce with Africa would prosper without it. His position combines humanitarian argument with British commercial expansion. The book also records his involvement in Irving’s plantation using enslaved labor. Chapter XI; Chapter XII. Describes violent capture, compelled displacement, and continued ownership. His manuscript supplies testimony about enslavement, but it does not present Equiano’s program of parliamentary abolition and alternative commerce. Complete Bird translation.
Reading the surviving text An adult author selects, orders, and interprets earlier experiences for publication. His documents, recollections, religious explanations, and arguments are different kinds of evidence within one book. “Written by Himself” does not make the text an unmediated record. Translation changes interpretation. Where Bird writes “great necessity,” Lo and Ernst translate “great harm.” Their notes also identify Islamic theological quotations in passages earlier readers treated as straightforward Christian testimony. Bird; Lo and Ernst, section B.2 and notes.

The comparison uses Equiano’s whole work, not only Chapters III–IV. Omar’s original dates to 1831; Isaac Bird’s English translation is later and undated. The complete modern edition by Mbaye Lo and Carl W. Ernst was deposited in UNC’s repository in 2022. Its editorial divisions help distinguish repeated passages and translation decisions.

Translation is part of the comparison. Equiano composed his book in English; the English words attributed to Omar pass through a translator. Identify the translator and edition when quoting him.

Sources and limits

The comparison concerns the works as a whole; it is not limited to Equiano’s chapters III–IV. Descriptions of a text’s strategy are interpretations supported by the cited passages. They do not establish an author’s private motives.
