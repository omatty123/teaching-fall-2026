Rush gets a “we” for free. Naquin spent 20 years trying to build one.

Chief Albert Naquin wanted the Army Corps of Engineers to pay for the islanders to move together. Chris Brunet wanted to know why the Corps had left the island outside the levee. On p. 32, Naquin tells Rush that people who lived off the island raised questions at the meeting with the government. I wanted to hear those questions and what it would have taken to get people to agree. Rush turns to the history of the wetlands just as Naquin gets to the hard part of organizing. Rush, pp. 31–33

Bengal’s sharecroppers1946

In the Tebhaga movement, communist organizers and sharecroppers demanded two-thirds of the harvest for the grower and the right to thresh the grain on the grower’s own floor. That second demand mattered. Once the grain was on the landlord’s floor, he controlled the division. Farmers across caste and religious lines could agree on who should control the grain they grew. They pushed Bengal’s government to publish a bill protecting their share. The government never passed it, and police fired on the farmers. Benoy Konar on the two demands

Vietnam’s independence coalition1945

In August 1945, the communist-led Việt Minh brought delegates from political parties and religious organizations to Tân Trào. They agreed on what independence would mean for a tenant who owed rent, a farmer in debt, or a woman who wanted to vote. Their program called for lower rents, postponed debt payments, and universal suffrage. Later that month, several Việt Minh members gave up their seats in the provisional government so people outside the front could help govern. Carrie Bradshaw and the royal “we”pp. 24, 90–91

The oil companies and the islanders

In Sex and the City, something happens to one of Carrie Bradshaw’s friends. By the time Carrie sits down to write her column, she’s wondering what it means for all of us. Listen for that voice in Rising. On p. 24, oil companies cut channels through the marsh. People on Isle de Jean Charles live with the damage. By the end of the paragraph Rush is talking about “our very ideas of who we are.” The islander, the oil executive and the visiting writer now share a pronoun. Only the company had an obligation to fill in the channel. Carrie’s column and voice-over · Rising, p. 24

What Rush asks Hal Wanless

On p. 90, Rush asks Hal Wanless, “What comes next?” Then the voice-over starts. “If I believe you, will I be among the chosen?” Then she wonders whether the most vulnerable people will have the same chances. Hal is sitting right there. She could ask him who gets to leave, who gets to stay, and who decides. She lets the interview move on. The reader learns that Rush worried about those people. We still don’t know what Hal would have said. Rising, pp. 90–91

Who is this written for?Audience and reception

The reader Rush pictured

When a college student asked whom she imagined reading Rising, Rush described a white weekend hiker with an REI membership who already read Orion. She included herself. She wanted that reader to take inequality seriously and to hear from people whose politics they might dismiss. She also wanted a place beside literary essayists such as Eula Biss and Maggie Nelson. The islanders are among the people she listens to. The reader she imagines persuading starts somewhere else. Rush on her readers · Rush on her literary ambitions

The route into a classroom

Rush earned a Master of Fine Arts in nonfiction at Southern New Hampshire University. Guernica published her Maine marsh reporting in 2017. Milkweed Editions published Rising in 2018. It became a 2019 Pulitzer finalist in General Nonfiction. UC Santa Barbara then selected it for a campus-wide reading program. Each institution made a choice about which account of the coast more people would read. Our class has made that choice, too. MFA · Magazine piece · Pulitzer citation · Campus selection

What the praise singles out

David Biello called Rising “a book about language, first and foremost” in The New York Times Book Review. He found the residents’ accounts the most intense parts of the book. Boston’s NPR member station WBUR interviewed Rush on Radio Boston. Its headline foregrounded retreat as unavoidable. Times review · Review excerpt · WBUR interview

Milkweed quotes The Nation praising Rush’s “lyrical, deeply reported essays” and their attention to vulnerable communities. Ecofascism

The trouble with “we.” The oil companies cut the channels on p. 24. By p. 35 Rush is wondering whether the islanders understand the harm they do by driving to buy groceries. That’s where I hear misanthropy creeping in. The company can agree that we’re all guilty and still walk away from the canal it was supposed to fill. Murray Bookchin on blaming humanity

I’d put these two ideas side by side: “We’re a scum species” and “someone should do something for the most vulnerable.” On pp. 53–54, nearly eight billion of us share the blame. On pp. 90–91, Rush wonders whether the most vulnerable will have the same chances. The reader gets to feel guilty for belonging to the species and decent for caring about its victims. Meanwhile, the people we’re feeling sorry for have demands of their own. Chief Albert Naquin named exactly who should do what. He wanted the Army Corps of Engineers to pay for the islanders to move together. Rush’s interview, pp. 90–91 · Naquin’s demand, pp. 31–33

Fewer people. On p. 74 Rush reads south Florida’s population figures alongside God’s judgment of human wickedness in Genesis, even as she rejects the idea that the flood is divine punishment. Having more neighbors starts to sound like a moral failure. Degrowth can mean cutting oil production in wealthy economies. Calling for fewer people takes us somewhere else. I see an ecofascist danger when someone claims the right to decide whose families may grow or who must leave to save the coast. What degrowth advocates propose

The “noble savage” stereotype. On pp. 28–29 Rush introduces whole societies, then says “most” of their villages were camps people could move when the river rose. Three pages later she mentions Cahokia. People built a city there, with homes and monumental earthworks. In Louisiana, the Chitimacha describe how they governed their villages. Moving camp during a flood tells us how people handled a flood. The stereotype is the flattering story that Native people lived in harmony with nature because they could always pack up and leave. Rush’s “we,” pp. 7, 53–55. On p. 7 we are making the sixth mass extinction. On p. 53 Rush compares the speed at which nearly eight billion people are changing the planet to the asteroid impact that killed the dinosaurs. Look at the list of what those people are doing. Getting fed, clothed, and housed goes in the same sentence as getting distracted. On p. 54 she names the Capitalocene and says “certain human beings” dominate. Within a few lines all of us are opening the earth’s veins. On p. 55 she imagines what would be left of us in the fossil record.

Food and population, pp. 35, 39, 67, 71–74. Chris drives to buy food his family used to grow. Edison says more neighbors make it easier to get what you need and recover after a storm. On p. 67 it is Laura Sewall who wonders whether being sure of food and civil order dulls our senses. In Florida, Rush puts population growth beside drained wetlands, strip malls, cheap labor, and plastic waste. She explicitly rejects the idea that the flood punishes human sin on p. 74. The sequence still makes having more neighbors sound like another kind of damage.

Whose family gets a history, pp. 28–33, 39–41, 63–64, 95–96. The Mississippi moves. It takes another step to turn that fact into a story about what Native people are like. On p. 29 whole societies appear through camps that could move. In Maine, Rush traces European settlement through Laura Sewall’s family and even gives us the beach that carries its name. Read Chris Brunet’s family history just as closely. His grandfather built a house to last. Chris raised it twice. Albert Naquin wanted neighbors to move together after the island was left outside the levee. Edison wanted to stay. On p. 41 his attachment to the island is precisely what Rush wants the reader to feel. In the space of a chapter Native people have come to stand for the wisdom of moving and then for the meaning of staying. In Florida Dan Kipnis can sell early and make a profit. Each can see the water rising. They have different things to lose. The Tribal Council’s account

Whose world, pp. 4, 52, 56, 68–69. On p. 52 Rush says what “we” once knew about a changing earth, then identifies people who dated creation from the Bible as “Westerners.” On p. 56 a claim about Western fears of swamps becomes an explanation for why wetlands were overlooked. And yet Rush herself recounts a Hindu story of the earth’s renewal on a four-billion-year cycle. That is a different way of imagining deep time. Anyone raised with that story has a different starting point from the one Rush assigns to “us.” Faharul in Bangladesh also knows his own land is changing. His failing mustard crop gives him reason to consider leaving. On p. 71 Thailand enters the Florida story as a population statistic. Asian people and traditions appear in the book. They seldom get to define the “we.” Elizabeth Rush, Rising

Monkeywrenching

In environmental politics, monkeywrenching means deliberately interfering with or damaging property to stop work activists consider destructive. Edward Abbey’s 1975 novel The Monkey Wrench Gang helped popularize the term. Its characters see development in the American Southwest as a public wrong, even when the work has official approval. Abbey and the history of environmental protest

Rush, pp. 62–63. Laura Sewall raises the possibility of acting directly over a ditch plug in the Maine marsh. She knows the place and names a particular intervention. Within a page, Rush has moved from Sewall’s proposal to her own uncertainty about marshes worldwide. Notice how the change in scale changes who gets the last word.

Wendell Berry

Berry is a Kentucky farmer, poet, and essayist. In The Unsettling of America (1977), he argues that industrial agriculture changes who can earn a living on the land, who controls the work, and what a community knows how to do. That makes him useful on p. 35 as well. The drive for groceries has a history in how people lost the ability to grow food at home. Berry’s book on farming

Rush, pp. 88–89. After Robert says he wanted to leave his Miami house to his children, Rush quotes the opening of Berry’s second Sabbath poem of 1988. Read one sentence further in each writer. Berry turns to what people have been entrusted to care for. Rush turns to what happens inside the mind when we imagine destruction. Read Berry’s poem

Recursion

A rule that calls itself. Each step replaces every straight piece with a bent one. Stop at any step and you have a drawing. Never stop and you have an infinite regression.

Coasts do this. Measure a shore with a shorter ruler and it comes out longer.

Two mirrors facing each other. Indra’s net, where every jewel reflects every other jewel.

Fractal

Click to zoom

z → z² + c. One rule, applied again to its own answer. Zoom as far as you like and it keeps going.

A recursive universe

Five cells, Conway’s rules. Each generation is made only from the one before. William Poundstone’s The Recursive Universe (1985) builds on this.

A universe looking back

A “self-excited circuit.” John Wheeler’s proposal: the universe produces observers, and observers give even its earliest days their reality. It is speculative and no one has completed it.
