What we saw

The Chicago canal, and the electrification site

Until 1900 the Chicago River carried the city’s sewage into Lake Michigan, the same lake Chicago drank from. In 1891 typhoid killed 1,997 Chicagoans, the worst rate of any major city in the United States or Europe (Egan, p. 262).

The Sanitary and Ship Canal fixed that by sending the river the other way. It also joined the Great Lakes to the Mississippi basin. Egan calls it the lakes’ “back door,” and the only thing between Asian carp and Lake Michigan is an electric barrier “with a history of unexpected shutdowns” (Egan, p. xviii).

What we learned

Alexis de Tocqueville, in Green Bay

On August 9, 1831, the steamship Superior, carrying about 200 passengers, reached Green Bay. Alexis de Tocqueville went ashore at Fort Howard and interviewed the fort’s commander.

He wanted to learn all he could about Native Americans: the principles they lived by, and why those brought them into conflict with Anglo-Americans (Clarke Historical Library, Central Michigan University). The marker in Green Bay was placed in 1997-98 by C-SPAN and the cable industry, who were retracing his route.

What we read

Waukesha, center of the world for a little while

In the Gilded Age Waukesha’s springs made it the “Saratoga of the West,” a resort for the sick and the fashionable. It began in 1868, when Richard Dunbar started selling Bethesda Spring water, and visitors included Mary Todd Lincoln (Egan, pp. 259, 261). In 1892 a Hygeia Spring owner tried to pipe the water to the Chicago World’s Fair. Waukesha met his train with militia and a cannon (Egan, pp. 260-263).

Then the springs failed. Egan writes that all but a handful of the springs have vanished and the city was pulling radium-laced water from wells 2,000 feet down, about three times the federal limit (p. 264). Waukesha asked for 10.1 million gallons a day and the Great Lakes governors approved it in 2016 (p. 275). The approved average is 8.2 million gallons a day (Circle of Blue). Lake Michigan water arrived on October 9, 2023, through a 35-mile pipeline, and treated water goes back by the Root River.

What we asked

Chicago as the most resilient city, after the apocalypse

AlphaGeo’s March 2026 report rates 72 of the world’s largest cities on climate risk and resilience. Chicago comes out as the most resilient, thanks to low coastal and tropical hazard exposure together with strong urban infrastructure.

Two things are working at once: where the city sits, and what it built. The Deep Tunnel is one of the things it built. The New York Times ran a piece on how prepared your city is on September 3.
