CABIN in practice: Genesis 6–9

Use the same revision moves we need in the Egan summaries. Read the weak version, check the passage, and revise it before revealing one possible improvement.

Five revision problems. Answers hidden.

Instructor-written examples—not quotations from student papers. Each row practices one revision move.
From the Egan summariesWeak Genesis versionOne stronger versionCheck it in the reading
C

Complete

Leaving out a step or complication essential to the chosen chapter or process.

“Noah survives the flood. God promises not to destroy all life by flood again. The story ends happily.”

It stops before the assigned reading does—and supplies a verdict on the ending.

“God promises not to repeat the destruction, although human evil remains. Noah’s story continues through a family conflict, his curse of Canaan, and his death.”

For a summary of Genesis 6–9 as a whole, include the ending that changes the account. A narrower selected process has a different scope.

8:21; 9:18–29

Name two events after the covenant that the weak version leaves out.

A

Accurate

Combining a real detail with the wrong cause, person, or moment.

“God curses Ham for seeing Noah naked.”

A recognizable incident, but both the speaker and the target of the curse are wrong.

“After Ham sees Noah naked and tells his brothers, Noah curses Ham’s son Canaan.”

Keep the unexplained shift visible. Do not invent an action by Canaan to fill the gap.

9:22–25 · family diagram

Who acts? Who speaks? Who is cursed?

B

Brief

Spending words on repeated setup instead of the main action and evidence.

“After the flood had ended and Noah had come out of the ark, he proceeded to build an altar and make an offering.”

The event is correct. The setup takes more words than it needs.

“After leaving the ark, Noah builds an altar and makes an offering.”

Same sequence, fewer words. Brevity does not require deleting a useful hook or an important complication.

8:18–20

Cut the sentence in half while preserving who does what and when.

I

Independent

Assuming the reader already knows the people, section, or claim.

“He promises them it will never happen again.”

Who? To whom? What will not happen? The sentence relies on context it has not supplied.

“In Genesis 9, God promises Noah, his descendants, and all living creatures that a flood will never again destroy all flesh.”

Give an unfamiliar reader enough context. Clear referents matter more than a prescribed opening formula.

9:8–17 · covenant

Replace “he,” “them,” and “it” with the passage’s actual people and promise.

N

Neutral

Adding praise, blame, or a conclusion that the source does not state.

“God finally realizes his cruel plan was a failure.”

“Cruel” judges the action; “realizes … a failure” supplies an unreported conclusion.

“Before and after the flood, the text describes human hearts as evil; afterward, God promises not to repeat the destruction.”

The tension remains visible without adding an explanation of God’s motives.

6:5–7 and 8:21–22

What remains the same? What promise changes the response?

Distinguishing summary from analysis

Noticing that human evil remains is already an analytical act. A summary can report that pattern. An analysis goes further: what does that repetition suggest about the flood’s purpose? Distinguish what the passage says from the interpretation you build from it.

These are sentence-level revisions, not complete model summaries. The Egan assignment permits a chapter or a process: completeness is measured within that chosen scope. A question, scene, or allusion can be an effective opening.
