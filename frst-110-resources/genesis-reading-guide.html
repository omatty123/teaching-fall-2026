Getting oriented

Genesis is the first book of the Jewish Torah, the five books that also begin the Christian Old Testament. These chapters tell the story of Noah and the flood. Familiarity with the Bible is not assumed.

How to find a passage: in Genesis 8:21, 8 is the chapter and 21 is the verse. The large numbers in your pamphlet start chapters; the small numbers mark verses. Cite those numbers rather than the pamphlet page. The reading begins on PDF page 15 and ends partway down PDF page 18; chapter 10 on that last page is beyond Monday’s assignment.

God / the LORD: these refer to the same God within this story. Small-capital LORD is a translation convention for the Hebrew divine name, written YHWH. The change in wording does not introduce another character. About the name

The family: Noah and his wife; their sons Shem, Ham, and Japheth; and the three sons’ wives. Eight people enter the ark. The women are included but not named in these chapters. Canaan, important at the end, is Ham’s son and Noah’s grandson.

Reading a footnote: “Heb” means Hebrew, the language of this text; “Gk” means Greek. “Meaning of Heb uncertain” and alternatives such as “Or window” mark a translation difficulty. They are not additional events in the story.

Chapter 6 · The decision and the instructions

6:1–4 · The strange opening

“Sons of God” is often understood here as heavenly beings; interpreters have also proposed human groups. It is not a reference to Jesus. Nephilim names mysterious figures associated with ancient heroes and, in later interpretation, giants. These brief verses assume a background they do not explain fully. You have not missed an earlier definition in the assigned reading. Notes on 6:1–4

The 120 years (6:3): interpreters disagree over whether this limits human lifespan or announces the time remaining before the flood. Noah’s much greater age later in the story is a real difficulty for a simple lifespan reading. Keep the two numbers distinct; 120 is not Noah’s age when the rain begins. NET translation note

6:5–13 · Why a flood? Why Noah?

The narrator identifies human wickedness, corruption, and violence. God regrets making humanity and decides on destruction. “All flesh” can include animals as well as humans: check the surrounding list each time the phrase appears.

“Righteous” and “blameless” describe Noah’s upright conduct. “Walked with God” describes his relationship with God, rather than giving a walking route. Notice the qualification “in his generation” in 6:9. The narrator singles out Noah; it does not separately explain the moral character of each person in his household.

Check the text: Find the stated reason for the flood and the description that distinguishes Noah. These are two different questions.

6:14–22 · Building and provisioning the ark

Ark
The vessel that will preserve life through the flood. Here the instructions concern its compartments, dimensions, covering, and supplies.
Cubit
An ancient measure based on the forearm, roughly elbow to fingertip. The measurements describe a very large vessel; you do not need to convert them to follow the story.
Pitch
A waterproof coating used to seal the vessel.
Cypress / gopher wood
Your pamphlet says “cypress,” with a note that the Hebrew is uncertain. Another translation may leave the word as “gopher.” The exact wood is disputed.
Covenant
A solemn commitment establishing a relationship. God announces one in 6:18; chapter 9 explains its promise, participants, and sign.

For cubit, wood, and the uncertain roof/window wording: translation notes on 6:14–16.

Food is part of the instructions (6:21). The story is concerned with sustaining the people and animals, as well as getting them aboard.

Chapter 7 · Entering the ark and the flood

7:1–10 · Two animals, or seven pairs?

Clean / unclean are religious classifications of animals, not descriptions of dirt or health. The distinction matters when Noah later offers animals in sacrifice (8:20). In your translation, 7:2 calls for seven pairs of clean animals and one pair of unclean animals; 7:3 also specifies seven pairs of birds. Compare the instruction about two of every kind in 6:19–20.

Why do instructions and events seem to repeat?

You have noticed a feature of the text. Many biblical scholars understand the flood account as a combination of traditions preserved together. Repeated instructions, different animal counts, divine names, and chronological details are among the evidence they discuss. Scholars debate the history of composition. For this reading, identify the actual differences before deciding how to explain them. A textual study of the flood’s parallel accounts

7:11–24 · Where the water comes from

“The great deep” refers to waters below the earth in the ancient picture of the world. “Windows of the heavens” describes openings above. The flood is presented as water breaking in from below and above, as well as falling as rain. Note on 7:11

Noah’s age: 7:6 says he is 600. That is what this narrative reports; it is not a typo. Read it as part of the story’s depiction of an exceptionally long-lived early humanity.

Forty days / 150 days: 7:12 names forty days of rain; 7:24 names 150 days during which the waters prevail. Neither number by itself gives the whole time aboard. Chapter 8 continues the waiting and drying. Track what each number measures before treating the numbers as interchangeable.

Check the text: Who closes the ark in 7:16? What living things does 7:21–23 explicitly include in the destruction?

Chapter 8 · Receding water and a changed promise

8:1–19 · Remembering, waiting, and testing

“God remembered Noah” marks a turn toward action on behalf of Noah and the animals: a wind follows and the waters begin to recede. The sentence does not say that God had forgotten where the ark was.

Ararat: the text says “mountains of Ararat,” a mountain region, rather than identifying one exact summit. The ark comes to rest before Noah receives the instruction to leave. Landing, seeing dry ground, and disembarking are distinct moments. Note on 8:4

The birds are tests of conditions outside. Noah sends a raven, then a dove. Follow the dove’s three trips: it returns; it returns with an olive leaf; it does not return. The leaf is evidence within the action before it becomes a symbol a reader might already recognize.

8:20–22 · Sacrifice and the promise

Altar: a place for presenting offerings to a deity. A burnt offering is an animal sacrifice burned on the altar. Noah uses clean animals and birds, which helps explain why the clean/unclean distinction mattered earlier.

The pleasing smell leads into God’s speech. Human hearts are still described as evil, but God promises not to repeat the destruction and affirms the continuing cycles of seasons, farming, and day and night.

Read two passages together: Compare 6:5–7 with 8:21–22. What does each say about humanity? What does God decide in each? Establish that contrast before explaining it.

Chapter 9 · Life after the flood

9:1–7 · Food, blood, and human life

“Be fruitful and multiply” means reproduce and populate the earth. The passage permits eating animals, prohibits consuming their blood with the meat, and requires an accounting for human bloodshed.

Lifeblood: blood is associated with life, which is why eating meat and consuming blood are treated differently. “A reckoning” means being held accountable. “Sheds blood” here concerns killing, not an accidental cut. Verse 9:6 connects the seriousness of killing a human being to humanity’s being made in God’s image. Notes on 9:4–6

9:8–17 · Who belongs to the covenant?

God makes a commitment to Noah, his descendants, and the living creatures. Descendants means the generations who come after someone. The promise extends beyond the people presently listening.

The bow in the clouds is the rainbow, the visible sign of the covenant. Read the verbs in 9:14–16 carefully: God says that God will see and remember. The stated promise concerns another flood that destroys all flesh; it does not promise that no place will ever flood or that no one will suffer again.

Check the text: List whom the covenant includes. Would “God makes a promise to Noah” give a reader its full scope?

9:18–29 · Noah, his sons, and Canaan

The reading continues after the covenant. Noah plants a vineyard, a place where grapes are grown, drinks wine, and becomes drunk. Ham sees his father naked and tells his brothers; Shem and Japheth cover Noah without looking.

Keep the generations straight: Noah → his son Ham → Ham’s son Canaan. Noah’s curse names Canaan, although Ham is the son whose conduct the preceding verses describe. Noah speaks the curse; the narrator does not present it as a new command from God.

If the punishment seems hard to explain, the difficulty is in the passage. It does not fully explain why Canaan receives the curse. Interpretations of Ham’s act and of the story’s background differ. Begin with the actions and relationships actually stated rather than supplying an unmentioned offense.

“Curse” here is a pronouncement of harm or subjection, not simply a swear word. The curse concerns servitude. The passage mentions no skin color. Later readers used the so-called “curse of Ham” to justify racial slavery; that is a history of interpretation, not a racial description supplied by these verses. About that later use

The final verse records Noah’s death. Unlike Uta-napishti in Gilgamesh, surviving the flood does not make Noah immortal.

Returning to Gilgamesh and to summary

Use specific scenes for comparison: the reason for destruction, the selection of the survivor, the birds, the sacrifice and its smell, and the survivor’s life afterward. Shared details do not automatically perform the same function in both stories.

For the sacrifice, place Genesis 8:20–21 beside Gilgamesh XI 161–163. First identify what happens in each scene; then compare the response to the offering.

Notice who is included and who is heard. Animals are explicitly included in the covenant. Noah’s wife and daughters-in-law survive, but these chapters give them no names or speeches. Those are features of how the narrative presents the event.

A flood raises a reasonable question about warning and response. A precise observation is: Genesis 6–9 records no speech in which Noah warns the people outside his household. That differs from claiming he never warned anyone or did not care about them. The first describes this account’s limits; the others make claims about what happened or what he felt that this account does not establish.
