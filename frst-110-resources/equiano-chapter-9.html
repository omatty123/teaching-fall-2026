Equiano · Chapters 9–10

FRST 110 · Friday, November 6 · Volume II, Chapters IX–X

Where Chapter 9 goes, 1767–1773

Seven voyages, now as a free man. Select one to follow it on the map.

  1. In St. Kitts a captain will not take him on board until he will “advertise” himself “like a slave.” Robert King writes him a certificate. Seven weeks later he is back in London.

  2. Hairdresser to Captain John Jolly on the Delawar. About five months in Smyrna, where he sees the Greeks “kept under by the Turks, as the negroes are in the West Indies by the white people.”

  3. Carnival time. The Inquisition searches the ship for Bibles; anyone hiding one could be “imprisoned and flogged, and sent into slavery for ten years.”

  4. Genoa’s marble is “disgraced by the galley slaves.” Vesuvius erupts at Naples and the ash lies on deck. Plague in Smyrna. Going home, a cook’s pan of fat nearly burns the ship.

  5. Steward on the Grenada Planter. A white buyer will not pay, and a justice of the peace gives free Black men no remedy. Equiano and three unpaid white sailors collect by force.

  6. Steward on the Jamaica. Cruel punishments; a buyer at Port Morant who threatens instead of paying; Sunday dances at Spring Path, Kingston, where Africans “retain most of their native customs.”

  7. With Dr. Irving on the Race Horse under Captain Phipps, looking for a passage to India. A fire in the tow and aquafortis off Shetland, sea-horses, nine bears, and eleven days frozen in the ice near 80° north.

In London between voyages he trains as a hairdresser in Coventry Court, Haymarket, learns the French horn, studies arithmetic in an evening school, and works for Dr. Charles Irving, who is making sea water fresh.

Lines show the order of ports, not exact courses. Place names and dates are from Equiano’s text.

Equiano · Chapter 10

After the Arctic voyage

Back in London, Equiano’s fear of death becomes a search for salvation. He attends churches, investigates Quaker, Catholic and Jewish religious life, and reads the Bible. He judges some Christians less honest than the Turks he has met and considers settling in Turkey. Chapter X

John Annis · April 4, 1774

Equiano recommends Annis as a cook aboard the Anglicania. William Kirkpatrick, whom Annis had left by consent, forcibly takes him from the ship in London. Equiano obtains a writ, brings a court officer, disguises himself by whitening his face, and seeks Granville Sharp’s help. He says his attorney takes his money but does nothing useful. Annis is carried to St. Kitts, tortured and dies. Chapter X

“I proved the only friend he had, who attempted to regain him his liberty if possible, having known the want of liberty myself.”

The love feast

A sailor and a minister introduce him to a religious gathering. Expecting a meal, he finds hymns, prayers, personal accounts of faith, and shared buns and water. He is especially struck by the speakers’ certainty that they are saved. He calls it his first soul feast. Chapter X

Good works and faith

Equiano thinks keeping eight of the Ten Commandments should count toward salvation. His new acquaintances tell him that salvation depends on Christ, not his record of good deeds. A minister compares one unforgiven sin to one leak sinking a ship. Equiano remains distressed, takes work on the Hope, and reaches Cadiz on September 23. Chapter X

Conversion · October 6, 1774

While reading Acts 4:12 at Cadiz, he describes seeing the crucified Christ with the eye of faith. He becomes convinced that salvation is God’s gift. He recalls the events of his life, beginning with his kidnapping, as evidence of God’s guidance and protection. His shipmates do not share his understanding of the experience. Chapter X

“the scriptures became an unsealed book”

Return to London · December 1774

A sermon by the Reverend Mr. Romaine helps him understand the distinction between human works and divine election. He is received into fellowship at Westminster Chapel. The chapter closes with a poem recounting his distress and conversion. Chapter X

“I and my works can nothing do”
Works
Human actions, including good deeds and religious observance.
Grace
God’s freely given favor, rather than a reward earned by good deeds.
Providence
God’s guidance of events, as Equiano understands his life.

Source: Equiano, The Interesting Narrative (1789), Volume II, Chapter X. Quotations retain his wording.
