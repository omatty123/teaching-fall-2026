Flood displacement and levee labor, in voice, piano, and guitar. In Greenville, Mississippi, in 1927, Black families were told to stay on the levee and work. A refugee camp ran along the levee, the National Guard was called in, and officials would not let them evacuate. They worked without pay.

Bessie Smith, “Back-Water Blues”

Bessie Smith · touring stage and recording star

Raised in Chattanooga, Tennessee, Smith sang on street corners before joining a traveling show in 1912. She became a major theater performer and Columbia recording artist in the 1920s. Her shows combined song, comedy, and drama, and she recorded with musicians including Louis Armstrong and pianist James P. Johnson, who accompanies “Back-Water Blues.” Her career connects these songs to professional touring and jazz as well as the blues.

Source: National Park Service · Bessie Smith.

Alice Pearson, “Greenville Levee Blues”

Alice Pearson · a small surviving record

Pearson recorded six blues for Paramount in Chicago around July 1927, accompanied by pianist Freddie Coates. David Evans identifies this as her only recording session. Three songs address recent disasters: “Memphis Earthquake,” “Greenville Levee Blues,” and “Water Bound Blues.” A later musician’s recollection associates a singer named Alice and a pianist named Freddy with Greenville. Details of her life remain uncertain; Evans’s reconstruction of her personal flood experience is an inference from songs and testimony.

Source: David Evans, “High Water Everywhere,” pp. 39–40.

Lonnie Johnson, “Broken Levee Blues”

Lonnie Johnson · blues and jazz instrumentalist

Born in New Orleans into a musical family, Johnson played guitar and violin. He worked in St. Louis and on Mississippi riverboats, and began recording for OKeh in 1925. He recorded with Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington and made guitar duets with Eddie Lang. In 1929 he toured with Bessie Smith’s revue. His career joined blues singing, jazz ensembles, and studio work; the speaker in “Broken Levee Blues” should not automatically be treated as Johnson recounting his own arrest.

Source: Patrick Huber · Missouri Encyclopedia.

The Mississippi, the Ohio, and the Cumberland

Greenville, Helena, and Cairo are named in today’s songs. Nashville provides the flood background for Bessie Smith’s “Back-Water Blues.”

  1. Greenville, Mississippi · Alice PearsonFlood displacement and the levee camp after the April 1927 Mound Landing break north of the city.
  2. Helena, Arkansas · Lonnie JohnsonA Mississippi River town on the Arkansas bank; the speaker wants to return here.
  3. Cairo, Illinois · Lonnie JohnsonNear the meeting of the Ohio and Mississippi rivers; named in the song’s account of policing.
  4. Nashville, Tennessee · Bessie Smith’s backgroundOn the Cumberland River. David Evans connects the song to the December 1926 flood, before Smith’s February 1927 recording.
