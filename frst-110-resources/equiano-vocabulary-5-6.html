Feelings across the two chapters

Identify whose feeling is described, what causes it, and how it changes. Several feelings can coexist. Gratitude, resignation, and hope do not mean that enslavement becomes acceptable.

PassageLanguage and context
V · sold againcontrition → dejection → faint hope

He first interprets renewed enslavement as punishment for his own conduct. Religious self-blame, sorrow, and hope coexist.

V · leaving Englandanguish · tumultuous emotions · unutterable grief

Departure closes the possibility of rescue. The language intensifies beyond ordinary disappointment.

V · arrival and Kinghorror → relief → an aching heart

Reassurances reduce immediate fear, but watching the English ship depart renews the pain of separation.

V · living under Kingfortitude · gratitude · fear

He resolves to endure. Favorable treatment and gratitude do not remove ownership or the danger of sale.

V · witnessing others’ sufferingpity · melancholy · despair

Some emotions belong to other enslaved people; the narrator also describes his own response to their suffering.

VI · stolen trading goodsconsternation · distress · indignation

Shock and suffering become anger at an injustice. Indignation names the moral judgment within the anger.

VI · Joseph Clipsonhorror · alarm · an anxious boding mind

The kidnapping makes legal freedom appear insecure. He fears future loss of liberty, not merely present hardship.

VI · preparing for Philadelphiahope · alacrity → astonishment

The prospect of buying freedom energizes work. The threat of another sale abruptly reverses his expectations.

VI · Farmer confirms his accountexquisite pain → fullest joy · gratitude

His emotions change rapidly when the captain supports him and King names a purchase price. This is a promise of possible freedom, not manumission yet.

VI · assault and carebenumbed · uneasy · weeping

Bodily numbness describes Equiano’s injuries. Uneasiness and tears describe Farmer’s response; identify whose feeling is being named.

Important distinctions: contrition concerns perceived wrongdoing; dejection concerns low spirits; despair concerns lost hope. Consternation is shocked confusion; indignation is anger at injustice. Fortitude is endurance, while alacrity is eager energy. “Indifferent” has two separate senses below.

Sources and scope

Both complete chapters were rescanned for this resource, including the opening and closing passages. This is a broad reading vocabulary, not a concordance of every word. It includes emotional language, moral and religious terms, power and law, commerce, seamanship, and older meanings. Definitions are written for these contexts.
