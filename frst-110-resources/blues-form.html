What are the blues?

Delta blues · October 14, 16 and 19

A verse is twelve bars and three lines

Count four beats to a bar. A blues verse runs twelve bars. The words come in three lines, four bars each:

Abars 1–4 · the singer states a line
Abars 5–8 · the same line again
Bbars 9–12 · a new line answers it

Inside each line there is a second answer. The singer leaves spaces between phrases. The piano or guitar can answer in those spaces while also accompanying the voice. Call and response.

    The usual chords: I, IV, V

    Under the words, the band usually moves through three chords. The names come from the scale: start on the home note and count up. The chord built on note 1 is I, the chord on note 4 is IV, and the chord on note 5 is V. Roman numerals let the same plan work in any key.

    Shown in E, a common key for blues guitar: I is E, IV is A, V is B.

    I feels like home. IV moves a step away. V pulls hardest back home. The common twelve-bar plan:

    Piano for the chord buttons; guitar shuffle for the pattern. Tempo changes take effect on the next beat.

    Players change the plan all the time: an early IV in bar 2, a V in bar 12 to lead into the next verse. Listen for the turn to IV at bar 5 and the pull of V at bar 9. That is where the B line lands.

    Charley Patton bends it: “High Water Everywhere, Part 2”

    Patton starts in A A B, but his A lines already change on the repeat (marked A′), and they change more each time. He talks between lines, and by the fourth verse the rhyme itself shifts. The form stretches but holds.
