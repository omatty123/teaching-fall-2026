The recording

Collins recorded the hymn at St. Paul’s Chapel at Columbia University. She sings a cappella—without instrumental accompaniment—with a choir of friends.

The unaccompanied voices make the language of grace, danger, and deliverance especially clear. The words were written by John Newton in 1772. The familiar melody was joined to them decades later.

John Newton and the slave trade

Newton remembered surviving a storm at sea as a turning point in his faith. His religious awakening coexisted with continued participation in the slave trade.

1748–1754
After the storm and religious awakening in 1748, Newton continued working on slave ships, including as captain, through 1754.
1772–1773
He wrote “Amazing Grace” in December 1772 for his New Year’s Day sermon in 1773, while serving as a minister in Olney.
1779
The words appeared in Olney Hymns, a collection by Newton and William Cowper.
1788
Newton published Thoughts Upon the African Slave Trade, condemning the trade in which he had participated.

Equiano: belief and conduct

At the end of chapter 2, Equiano addresses “nominal Christians”: people who profess Christianity while buying human beings and separating families. He judges their conduct against the faith they claim.

Newton’s continued work in the slave trade after his religious awakening gives that contradiction a historical example. Personal religious change could occur while participation in exploitation continued.

Water also connects the accounts. Newton’s remembered storm concerns his own survival and faith. Equiano’s shipboard account describes the confinement and terror of people being carried across the Atlantic against their will.

The hymn concerns grace and salvation. Its later association with abolition draws on Newton’s history and on the uses people have made of the song.
