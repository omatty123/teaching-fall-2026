Four starting examples

A selected starting collection, not a complete inventory. These works make different kinds of claims about the past: firsthand testimony, historical reconstruction, and invented stories are not interchangeable.

Roots

Alex Haley · book, 1976
Television miniseries, 1977

Family saga and dramatization

Setting and story

Kunta Kinte’s story moves from The Gambia through capture and the Atlantic crossing to enslavement in North America. Later generations continue the family’s story.

Scenes in Africa · Scenes on the ship

Relation to evidence

Haley combined family oral tradition, research, and imaginative reconstruction. The book and series are representations of the past, not a surviving autobiography by Kunta Kinte.

Smithsonian book record · Library of Congress on the adaptation

Amistad

Steven Spielberg · film, 1997

Historical drama

Setting and story

The 1839 revolt and subsequent American court case involving Africans abducted from Sierra Leone. Their Atlantic crossing to Cuba preceded the later voyage aboard La Amistad.

Scenes in Africa · Scenes on the ship

Relation to evidence

A later dramatization of historical events, not a narrative written by one of the captives. Surviving court records provide a separate body of evidence about the case.

Official film page · National Archives documents

Twelve Years a Slave

Solomon Northup · memoir, 1853
Steve McQueen · film, 2013

Memoir and adaptation

Setting and story

Northup was a free Black New Yorker kidnapped in 1841 and enslaved in Louisiana until 1853. The film’s title is 12 Years a Slave.

Northup in a slave-ship manifest

Relation to evidence

Northup’s memoir was produced with editor David Wilson. The film adapts that published testimony. The narrated experience, its written presentation, and the screen adaptation remain distinguishable.

Library of Congress on the memoir · Official film page

Django Unchained

Quentin Tarantino · film, 2012

Fictional Western

Setting and story

An invented story set in the American South before the Civil War. Django works to rescue his wife, Broomhilda, from enslavement.

Relation to evidence

The film uses the conventions of a violent revenge Western. Its scenes are not documentary evidence of particular historical events, even when they depict historical institutions.

American Film Institute · Film record and classification

Autobiography, biography, and fiction

In autobiography, a person narrates their own life. A biography recounts someone else’s life. Fiction can draw on historical evidence without claiming that its story is an individual’s testimony.

Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative

1789 · Autobiography

Our course text presents childhood, captivity, Atlantic transportation, enslavement, and freedom as the author’s experiences. The autobiographical basis of the African childhood and crossing is disputed. Read the evidence about his origins.

Oxford introduction · Our chapter guide

Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave

1845 · Autobiography

Douglass describes enslavement in Maryland, literacy, resistance, and escape. The title emphasizes his authorship: “Written by Himself.”

Library of Congress · accounts and complete texts

Harriet Jacobs, Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl

1861 · Autobiography

Published under the name Linda Brent, Jacobs’s account gives particular attention to sexual coercion and the struggle for freedom.

National Archives · Harriet Jacobs papers

David W. Blight, Frederick Douglass: Prophet of Freedom

2018 · Biography

A historian reconstructs Douglass’s life from writings, newspapers, correspondence, and other records. This is a later author’s account of Douglass, distinct from Douglass’s own autobiographies.

Publisher · book and sources

Toni Morrison, Beloved

1987 · Novel

Through Sethe and her family, Morrison explores enslavement’s continuing effects after escape. Historical material informs the novel; its characters and narrative are not a transcript of one person’s testimony.

Publisher · book and historical context

Our discussion

Why does it matter to make distinctions between different types of slavery, especially comparing present to the past, and chattel slavery?

Distinctions help us explain how coercion works: the control claimed over another person, its enforcement, whether status passes to children, and the possibility of leaving or obtaining freedom. These are historical and institutional differences, not a ranking of whose suffering matters.

Chattel slavery
People are treated as property that can be bought, sold, or inherited. In British North America, this developed into a racial system of permanent, hereditary bondage targeting Africans and their descendants. “Chattel” identifies property status; racial and hereditary rules specify how this particular system operated. National Park Service.
Slavery in international law
The 1926 definition concerns the exercise of powers associated with ownership. Slavery can exist in practice even when no law recognizes someone’s ownership of another person. Legal abolition does not by itself eliminate enslavement. United Nations · definition and legal history.
Forced labour and trafficking
Forced labour involves work imposed through threatened penalties without voluntary consent, including freedom to leave. Adult trafficking involves acts such as recruitment or harbouring, coercive or deceptive means, and a purpose of exploitation. These categories overlap with slavery but are not interchangeable. International Labour Organization.
“Modern slavery”
An umbrella term covering several forms of exploitation, rather than one precise international legal category. The ILO’s global estimates group forced labour and forced marriage under this heading. The label does not mean that every included situation reproduces historical chattel slavery. ILO report · terminology, p. 13.

Equiano’s comparison

Equiano distinguishes slavery in the community he describes from West Indian slavery. He also describes captive-taking, sale, kidnapping, and unequal status in that community. His account gives evidence for comparison; it does not make African slavery uniform or harmless. Chapter 1.

A useful comparison names the place, period, legal status, means of coercion, and source. Similarities can reveal continuing exploitation; differences prevent us from treating distinct institutions and experiences as identical.

Sources and scope

Sources are linked beside each entry. This selected collection begins with four works already named and five further examples; it is not an inventory of all narratives of slavery. Publication dates identify the original works, not every later edition. Film depictions and fictional scenes remain distinct from surviving testimony and records.
