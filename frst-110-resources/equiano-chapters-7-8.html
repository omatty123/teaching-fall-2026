Equiano · Chapters 7–8

FRST 110 · Wednesday, November 4 · Volume II, Chapters VII–VIII

Equiano buys his freedom, then learns what a free black man can and cannot count on in Savannah. In Chapter VIII the ship he helps to steer is wrecked on the Bahama bank.

Map

Montserrat: he buys his freedom from Robert King, July 10, 1766. Savannah: as a free man he is threatened with flogging twice and nearly kidnapped. Antigua: after the Captain dies at sea, Equiano brings the sloop in and is called Captain. The Bahama bank: the wreck, the key, the search for water, and the rescue at New Providence. Eight words

manumissionThe legal act, and the paper, that frees an enslaved personVII: he goes to the Register Office to get his “manumission drawn up”
obeisanceA bow or gesture of respect to a superiorVII: “I made my obeisance to my master”
principalThe original sum of money, as distinct from the interest it earnsVII: “here is now the principal at last”
dominionOwnership and control over someone or somethingVII: “all right, title, dominion, sovereignty, and property”
appellationA name or title by which someone is calledVII: “a new appellation … which was Freeman”; later he is called Captain
imposeTo take unfair advantage of someone; to cheat or deceiveVII: “I had no notion that he intended thus to impose on me”
instrumentA person through whom something is done; an agentVIII: “the principal instrument in effecting our deliverance”
deliveranceRescue from danger or captivityVIII: used for the rescue at sea, and in religious language for being saved

Questions and passages

1. Can you buy your freedom without agreeing that you were property?

“Have you got forty pounds sterling?”Robert King at breakfast · VII
“you have received good interest for it all this time, and here is now the principal at last.”the Captain to King · VII
“all right, title, dominion, sovereignty, and property, which, as lord and master over the aforesaid Gustavus Vassa, I had”the manumission paper · VII
“expresses the absolute power and dominion one man claims over his fellow”why Equiano prints the paper in full · VII

2. What is freedom worth where the law will not protect you?

“I knew there was little or no law for a free negro here”Savannah · VII
“I would sooner die like a free man, than suffer myself to be scourged by the hands of ruffians”hiding from Mr. Read · VII
“I asked why? and if there was no law for free men?”the watch-house · VIII
“the other answered that I talked too good English.”two would-be kidnappers · VIII

3. Does gratitude bind a free man?

“none but the generous mind can judge of my feelings, struggling between inclination and duty.”King and the Captain ask him to stay · VII
“so I consented to slave on as before.”after the Captain breaks his word on the bullocks · VII
“had it pleased Providence that he had died but five months before, I verily believed I should not have obtained my freedom when I did”the Captain’s death · VII

4. Who saves the ship: God, Equiano, or the men at the oars?

“I dreamt the ship was wrecked amidst the surfs and rocks, and that I was the means of saving every one on board”the first dream · VIII
“not one of the white men did any thing to preserve their lives”rowing to the key · VIII
“I could not help looking on myself as the principal instrument in effecting our deliverance”the dream fulfilled · VIII
“through the mercy of God, we got again from the shoals into deep water”the second storm · VIII

5. What does a free man owe the people in the hold?

This passage describes enslaved people about to be shut in below deck.

“we took in a live cargo, as we call a cargo of slaves.”St. Eustatius · VII
“The captain immediately ordered the hatches to be nailed down on the slaves in the hold”the wreck · VIII
“I thought that my sin was the cause of this, and that God would charge me with these people’s blood.”the wreck · VIII
“I recovered just as the people were about to nail down the hatches, perceiving which, I desired them to stop.”the wreck · VIII

Foreshadowing

Three dreamsThree nights running he dreams the ship is wrecked “and that I was the means of saving every one on board.” After the rescue: “it was fulfilled in every part.” (VIII)
A curse at the pumpTired of pumping, he swears “Damn the vessel’s bottom out.” Hours later the sloop is “pierced and transfixed among the rocks,” and he reads the wreck as punishment for his oath. (VIII)
Navigation never taughtThe dying Captain is “very sorry he had not taught me navigation.” Equiano steers to Antigua by experience. The next captain “boasted strangely of his skill” and steers a new course onto the rocks. (VII–VIII)
The empty trunksThe silversmith’s treasure is “one dollar and a half”: “how uncertain and deceitful are all human affairs!” Soon after, a bullock butts the Captain, who never recovers. (VII)
Savannah, twice“Little or no law for a free negro here” in VII becomes the watch-house and the kidnappers in VIII. Chapter IX opens: “I determined never more to revisit it.”
Toward Chapter XHe vows never to swear again, tells the crew “swear not, but trust to God,” and leaves Georgia after acting “the parson for the first time in my life.” Friday’s reading is his conversion. (VIII)

Connections
