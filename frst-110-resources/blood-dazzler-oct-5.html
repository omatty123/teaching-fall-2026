Read the word notes

rain

Rain can be familiar and welcome before it becomes frightening. The same word carries the children’s play, an owner’s patience, and the storm’s change in scale.

  1. Only Everything I Own p. 8

    The homeowner waits patiently for rain beside the inherited house.

  2. Ghazal p. 16

    Children approach familiar rain; later mothers carry their lives to rooftops.

Characters: The homeowner, Children, Mothers

water

Water sustains life and threatens it. A household leaves drinking water for its dog; floodwater later reaches people stranded on a roof.

  1. Won’t Be but a Minute p. 10

    Luther B is left bowls of water while the household evacuates.

  2. Up on the Roof p. 23

    Water rises toward the feet of people waiting for rescue.

Characters: Luther B, The departing household, People on the roof

  • bodies: Water is already inside human bodies, yet floodwater threatens their protection. Compare pp. 19–20 with p. 23.
  • you: The roof poem connects direct address with rising water, making the survivor’s exposure the reader’s immediate point of view. Read p. 23.
wind

Wind becomes a perceptible force: it acquires color in one poem and becomes a wall against Luther B’s body in another.

  1. What Was the First Sound pp. 19–20

    Wind finds its color as the poem reconstructs the storm’s first sound.

  2. Luther B Rides Out the Storm p. 21

    Luther B presses his muzzle against a wall of wind.

Characters: Luther B, The storm

house

A house can be the center of an inherited life or one property among several. The contrast changes what evacuation puts at risk.

  1. Only Everything I Own p. 8

    The speaker identifies the house with a grandfather, garden, food, and daily routine.

  2. Inconvenient p. 13

    The affluent evacuee has a guest house, boat, car, and another home.

Characters: The homeowner, The affluent evacuee

  • leave: Leaving a structure also puts inherited routines, food, and belongings at risk. Compare pp. 7–8.
home

Home promises a place to return to. That promise differs for the household leaving its dog and the evacuee able to exchange one home for another.

  1. Won’t Be but a Minute p. 10

    The household promises to return home soon and retrieve Luther B.

  2. Inconvenient p. 13

    The speaker treats evacuation as exchanging home for its vacation version.

Characters: The departing household, The affluent evacuee, Luther B

my

This possessive pronoun changes with its speaker. A resident claims the materials of a life; Katrina claims her appetite, name, and power.

  1. Only Everything I Own p. 8

    Repeated my ties the house, garden, table, dust, and food to the homeowner.

  2. 8 a.m., Sunday, August 28, 2005 p. 11

    Katrina claims her mouth, name, fists, and swallowing eye.

Characters: The homeowner, Katrina

  • mouth: The resident’s possessive claims preserve a life; Katrina’s claims announce an appetite for buildings. Compare pp. 8 and 11.
money

Money can give people more choices, but it cannot guarantee protection. Compare the affluent evacuation with the money-spell poem’s fragile defense against weather.

  1. Inconvenient p. 13

    Related contrast: dollar accompanies the car and vacation home; money itself does not occur here.

  2. Voodoo II: Money p. 24

    The poem describes money as too thin to hold tomorrow back.

Characters: The affluent evacuee, The money-spell addressee

  • go: The same command meets unequal means of escape: compare the resident on p. 7 with the car and second home on p. 13.
go

The same evacuation command reaches people with unequal resources. Its apparent simplicity conflicts with the practical work of leaving.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    A resident answers the television order by questioning transport and refuge.

  2. Inconvenient p. 13

    The affluent speaker hears Go but can leave by car for another home.

Characters: The man on television, A resident, The affluent evacuee

  • money: The same command meets unequal means of escape: compare the resident on p. 7 with the car and second home on p. 13.
leave

Leaving involves specific losses and decisions. The word directs people away from their belongings and directs a household to abandon its dog temporarily.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    The order lists a house, food, a dog, a watch, and a church hat.

  2. Won’t Be but a Minute p. 10

    The departing household says to leave Luther B behind.

Characters: A resident, The departing household, Luther B

  • house: Leaving a structure also puts inherited routines, food, and belongings at risk. Compare pp. 7–8.
run

The command to run assumes movement is available. The wealthy evacuee has transport; the resident questions whether human bodies can meet the command.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    The television order says run; the resident questions its assumptions about bodies and transport.

  2. Inconvenient p. 13

    The speaker says the household runs while describing departure in a Lincoln.

Characters: The man on television, A resident, The affluent evacuee

eye

Katrina’s storm eye becomes a hungry observer. Another speaker imagines people inside that eye, reversing the question from what the storm sees to whom it threatens.

  1. 5 p.m., Thursday, August 25, 2005 p. 4

    Katrina describes an eye that craves sights and pushes her forward.

  2. Company’s Coming p. 14

    The storm survivor describes a threatening woman with us in her eye.

Characters: Katrina, The earlier storm survivor

  • eyes: Katrina’s hungry eye contrasts with the attention of human authorities. Compare p. 4 with the television man on p. 7 and president on p. 22.
mouth

A mouth can consume or admit danger. Katrina fills hers with buildings; a survivor advises keeping a human mouth closed against the weather.

  1. 8 a.m., Sunday, August 28, 2005 p. 11

    Katrina says she has filled her mouth with buildings.

  2. Company’s Coming p. 14

    The survivor tells the listener to keep a mouth closed so weather cannot enter.

Characters: Katrina, The earlier storm survivor

  • my: The resident’s possessive claims preserve a life; Katrina’s claims announce an appetite for buildings. Compare pp. 8 and 11.
bodies

Human bodies have limits that an evacuation instruction cannot remove. Their own water and protective bones become newly vulnerable as the storm arrives.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    The resident insists that bodies are only bodies, without wheels or gas.

  2. What Was the First Sound pp. 19–20

    The poem considers bodies already filled with water and secured by bone.

Characters: A resident, People exposed to the storm

  • water: Water is already inside human bodies, yet floodwater threatens their protection. Compare pp. 19–20 with p. 23.
eyes

Human eyes do not guarantee attention to human need. The television warning and the presidential performance expose different failures of recognition.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    The man’s gray eyes accompany a simple order; the resident questions whether he sees their limits.

  2. Gettin’ His Twang On p. 22

    The president’s dead eyes focus on the guitar chord while a woman voices abandonment.

Characters: The man on television, President George Bush, The woman in the Ninth

  • eye: Katrina’s hungry eye contrasts with the attention of human authorities. Compare p. 4 with the television man on p. 7 and president on p. 22.
see

Seeing means appetite for Katrina and a demand for recognition for the resident. Compare what each observer notices and what each overlooks.

  1. 5 p.m., Thursday, August 25, 2005 p. 4

    Katrina wants to see even the sound of weeping.

  2. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    The resident asks whether the television man can see the limits of their bodies.

Characters: Katrina, A resident, The man on television

  • know: The resident asks for recognition of familiar attachments and bodily limits. Read the uses of know and see together on p. 7.
sound

Sound crosses into sight and color. These impossible sensory combinations give the storm a presence that ordinary observation cannot contain.

  1. 5 p.m., Thursday, August 25, 2005 p. 4

    Katrina’s eye wants to see the sound of weeping.

  2. What Was the First Sound pp. 19–20

    The storm’s first sound is repeatedly described as purple.

Characters: Katrina, Mutts, Priestesses, Tree trunks

purple

Purple connects a familiar possession with an unfamiliar sensory event. The color moves from a church hat to the storm’s sound and wounded imagery.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    A purple church hat belongs to the life the resident is told to leave.

  2. What Was the First Sound pp. 19–20

    Purple describes the first sound through the image of a new wound.

Characters: A resident, The storm’s witnesses

name

A name can confer identity or authority. Katrina’s naming gives her force; the preacher’s claimed familiarity with Jesus supports his standing among the congregation.

  1. 11 a.m., Wednesday, August 24, 2005 p. 3

    Receiving a name gives Katrina pulse, temper, and a word in her own mouth.

  2. 10:30 a.m., Sunday, August 28, 2005 p. 17

    The congregation credits the preacher with knowing Jesus by his first name.

Characters: Katrina, The preacher, The congregation, Jesus

know

Knowing can mean familiarity with home or recognition of a threat. Katrina demands recognition; residents describe what makes an unfamiliar destination difficult to imagine.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    The resident says bodies remain tied to what they know.

  2. 8 a.m., Sunday, August 28, 2005 p. 11

    Katrina warns people that they must not know her name.

Characters: A resident, Katrina

  • see: The resident asks for recognition of familiar attachments and bodily limits. Read the uses of know and see together on p. 7.
God

God is invoked as commanding authority and as the destination of desperate protest. Religious language does not establish that protection arrives.

  1. Man on the TV Say p. 7

    The television order is compared with God directing people out of Paradise.

  2. Up on the Roof p. 23

    The roof survivor directs angry cries toward God while holding babies.

Characters: God, The man on television, People on the roof

you

The person being addressed changes. The prologue places you with a visitor drawn to the city; the roof poem places you inside exposure, judgment, and uncertain rescue.

  1. Prologue: And Then She Owns You pp. vii–viii

    A visitor is addressed as someone attracted and held by New Orleans.

  2. Up on the Roof p. 23

    The addressed person signals from a roof, holds babies, and faces rescue decisions.

Characters: A visitor, New Orleans, People on the roof

  • water: The roof poem connects direct address with rising water, making the survivor’s exposure the reader’s immediate point of view. Read p. 23.

Explicit characters

Explicit characters are named or directly described. They can be unnamed people, groups, animals, religious figures, or personified places and weather.

New Orleans and Katrina are different female figures. Keep the city’s voice separate from the storm’s voice, and keep the unnamed residents separate from one another.
