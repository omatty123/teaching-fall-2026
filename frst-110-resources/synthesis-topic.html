Synthesis: find your topic

Due Sunday, October 18, 5:00 p.m. · 500–750 words

Pick two works, or two parts of one work. Pick one theme they share. Then build one claim about what the pair shows that neither shows alone: one point of correspondence, not a list.

Themes and where they show up

Example

Work A: Charley Patton, “High Water Everywhere, Part 1”

Work B: Kansas Joe McCoy & Memphis Minnie, “When the Levee Breaks”

Theme: nowhere to go

Thesis: Both songs are about having nowhere to go, but Patton names town after town (Sumner, Leland, Greenville, Rosedale, Vicksburg) and finds water or a closed road at each, while McCoy and Memphis Minnie never name a place at all, only “no place to stay.” Read together, they show that the flood takes away more than a house: it takes away the map of where safety might be.

Lyrics and the map of Patton’s towns: Flood.

Questions to ask yourself

To build a thesis

  1. What is each author’s main point, stated fairly and fully, as in your summary?
  2. Who was each work made for (listeners in ancient Mesopotamia, record buyers in 1929, readers after Katrina), and what did the author want them to think, feel, or do?
  3. How does each author make the point: form, voice, repetition, who gets to speak?
  4. If the two authors met, where would they agree, and what would one push the other to see?
  5. What does one work reveal about the other that neither shows alone?
  6. What does your reader, a classmate who knows both works, probably assume, and how could your pairing change that?
  7. Where would a careful reader push back on your claim, and what evidence answers them?
  8. Having heard the authors and their audiences out, what do you think the pair shows, and why should it matter to someone outside this class?

To make the most of the assignment

  1. Which pairing would teach your classmates something they have not noticed yet?
  2. Which work do you keep returning to, and what pulls you back: the author’s craft, the people in it, the problem it raises?
  3. What one writing skill will this paper stretch (a sharper thesis, weaving quotations, transitions, an opening that pulls a reader in)?
  4. What did the feedback on your summary point to, and where will this paper show the change?
  5. Are you treating each author as someone making choices, not just a source of quotations?
  6. Read a paragraph aloud: does it sound like a person talking to a reader, or like a list?
  7. Who will read a draft before Sunday: a classmate, a writing tutor, Ma?
  8. Could this pairing grow into your Final Synthesis, beside The Interesting Narrative or Rising?
