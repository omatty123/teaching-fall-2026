Synthesis: find your topic

Due Sunday, October 18, 5:00 p.m. · 500–750 words

For Friday: choose the two or three things you will look at and the point you want to make.

Pick two works, or two parts of one work, and one theme they share. Then make one claim about something you can see only when you read them together. Stick to one point; don’t list everything they have in common.

Themes and where they show up

Ma’s example

Work A: Egan, Ch. 2–4: lampreys, alewives, mussels

Work B: Patricia Smith, Blood Dazzler

Theme: animals and other life

Thesis: Egan writes about alewives, lampreys, and mussels as species, while in Patricia Smith’s poems one dog, and even a hurricane, get a point of view. Read together, they show that we care more about one creature whose thoughts we can follow than about millions we only count, which matters to anyone who wants people to protect the Great Lakes, because we rarely fight for what we have never imagined from the inside.

Another example

Work A: Charley Patton, “High Water Everywhere, Part 1”

Work B: Kansas Joe McCoy & Memphis Minnie, “When the Levee Breaks”

Theme: nowhere to go

Thesis: Patton names town after town (Sumner, Leland, Greenville, Rosedale, Vicksburg) and finds water or a closed road at one after another, while McCoy and Memphis Minnie never name a place at all, only “no place to stay.” Read together, they show that a flood takes away more than a house; it takes away every safe place to run, which is worth remembering whenever someone asks why flood victims “just didn’t leave.”

Mock theses: from a feeling to an argument

Each one starts with a feeling and turns it into a thesis. Colors match the builder: Work A, Work B, and underlined: so what and who cares.

Gilgamesh and Egan

You feel: People knew and did nothing. It makes me furious.

Thesis: Gilgamesh finds a plant that could make him young again and loses it to a snake while he is swimming. Egan shows that Lake Erie was cleaned up in the 1970s and then got so polluted again that in 2014 people in Toledo were told not to drink their tap water. Both show people losing something they already had in hand, which matters to anyone who lives near the Great Lakes, because fixing a problem once does not mean it stays fixed.

Evidence: Gilgamesh 11.283–307 (Helle, pp. 110–111); Egan 224–227, 240.

The Quran and Patricia Smith

You feel: Nobody listened, and nobody came.

Thesis: In Sura 71, Noah’s people put their fingers in their ears so they don’t have to hear his warning. In Blood Dazzler, the president flies over the flooded city and sees a country “victimless, vast.” The Quran blames the people who ignore the warning, while Smith’s poems blame the people in charge, which matters because after a disaster it is easy to blame victims for staying instead of asking why help didn’t come.

Evidence: Qur’ān 71.5–7; P. Smith, “The President Flies Over” (36) and “Thankful” (48–49).

Genesis and Gilgamesh

You feel: Killing everyone to punish some is wrong, even when a god does it.

Thesis: In Genesis, God ends the flood by promising never again to destroy every living creature, while in Gilgamesh the god Ea scolds Enlil that “only the guilty should bear the guilt.” Both stories treat punishing everyone as a mistake, which matters because it shows that people thousands of years ago were already asking whether a ruler should punish a whole group for what some of them did.

Evidence: Gen. 8.21, 9.11; Gilgamesh 11.181–195 (Helle, pp. 106–107).

McCoy and Memphis Minnie, and Lonnie Johnson

You feel: The people who got flooded were forced to fix it.

Thesis: In “When the Levee Breaks,” the singer works on the levee “both night and day” and still can’t keep the water away, while in “Broken Levee Blues,” the police tell the singer to “work, fight, or go to jail.” Together, the songs show that the levee depended on the labor of people the water threatened, and Johnson shows that some of that labor was forced, which matters because it changes how we judge a disaster response: we have to ask who did the work and whether they had a choice.

Evidence: “When the Levee Breaks”; “Broken Levee Blues”; for more, Pearson, “Greenville Levee Blues.”

Genesis and Patricia Smith

You feel: The dead just turn into a number.

Thesis: Genesis carefully lists who gets on the ark, down to seven pairs of every clean animal, but everyone who drowns is covered in three short verses. Patricia Smith writes a whole poem, “34,” about the people who died at St. Rita’s nursing home. Smith gives the dead the attention Genesis does not, which matters because the way a story counts the dead shows whose lives it cared about.

Evidence: Gen. 7.2–3, 7.21–23; P. Smith, “34” (50–57).

Questions to ask yourself

To build a thesis

  1. What is each author’s main point, stated fairly and fully, as in your summary?
  2. Who was each work made for (listeners in ancient Mesopotamia, record buyers in 1929, readers after Katrina), and what did the author want them to think, feel, or do?
  3. How does each author make the point: form, voice, repetition, who gets to speak?
  4. If the two authors met, where would they agree, and what would one push the other to see?
  5. What does one work reveal about the other that neither shows alone?
  6. What does your reader, a classmate who knows both works, probably assume, and how could your pairing change that?
  7. Where would a careful reader push back on your claim, and what evidence answers them?
  8. Having heard the authors and their audiences out, what do you think the pair shows, and why should it matter to someone outside this class?

To make the most of the assignment

  1. Which pairing would teach your classmates something they have not noticed yet?
  2. Which work do you keep returning to, and what pulls you back: the author’s craft, the people in it, the problem it raises?
  3. What one writing skill will this paper stretch (a sharper thesis, weaving quotations, transitions, an opening that pulls a reader in)?
  4. What did the feedback on your summary point to, and where will this paper show the change?
  5. Are you treating each author as someone making choices, not just a source of quotations?
  6. Read a paragraph aloud: does it sound like a person talking to a reader, or like a list?
  7. Who will read a draft before Sunday: a classmate, a writing tutor, Ma?
  8. Could this pairing grow into your Final Synthesis, beside The Interesting Narrative or Rising?
