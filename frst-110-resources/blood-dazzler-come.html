How we reached “come”

Reviewing our October 7 reading · Patricia Smith, Blood Dazzler

The mysteryOur reasoningSmall reading actionsTest the answerResearch and ask Smith

The mystery: which verb?

In “Tankas,” a speaker says the soul is held in a word but does not name that word:

The breath just before
the last breath harbors the soul
encased in a verb.
I know the word by heart now.
Oh, I wish I could tell you.

“Tankas,” p. 38. Line breaks retained.

Our proposal: the text allows us to answer “come.” Smith does not explicitly identify it as the missing verb. We reached this interpretation by connecting evidence across poems.

Advanced analysis through small steps

Here is the reasoning behind our reading. Each step begins with something we can point to in the text.

  1. Identify exactly what is missing.

    The speaker specifies a verb, not just any word. The setting is the breath before death. That gives us two constraints: look for a verb connected with dying or crossing from life to death.

  2. Look nearby before looking elsewhere.

    Earlier in “Tankas,” the wind knows the speaker’s name and keeps calling. The unit ends “I come.” We have a candidate verb in the same poem, in a passage connecting a call with a threatened life.

  3. Notice when another poem makes the word conspicuous.

    In “Ethel’s Sestina,” Ethel says “They don’t hear Come.” The word then repeats on separate lines. Its repetition and isolation make it difficult to overlook. These extra lines expand the sestina’s usual pattern.

    “Ethel’s Sestina,” p. 46.

  4. Notice grammar as well as repetition.

    “I come” states a response; “Come.” can be a command or invitation. The same verb places us on different sides of a call. Ethel hears something others do not: the poem gives us access to her imagined experience.

  5. Follow the pattern into the next poem.

    “Didn’t Need No Music, Neither” begins with the promise that they would come and describes figures who arrive, call Ethel by her full name, and accompany her away. Coming, calling, naming, and departure occur together again.

    “Didn’t Need No Music, Neither,” p. 47; its dedication, “Again, for Mama Freeman,” explicitly connects it to Ethel.

  6. Make a claim about the relationship between the parts.

    We can read the later poems as helping us answer the earlier mystery. “Come” joins a summons with a response at the boundary between life and death. That makes it a plausible answer to the unnamed verb in “Tankas.”

We moved from identifying a part of speech to interpreting relationships across several poems. That is substantial analysis. The reasoning became more complex because we connected careful observations—not because we began with complicated vocabulary.

The small actions we can use again

Notice a feature, then explain what it contributes.
Count wordsCount repeated words and record where they occur. Distinguish exact repetition from related forms such as come and came. Frequency gives us a question to investigate; it does not prove significance by itself.
Read the directions inside the text“Encased in a verb” tells us what kind of word to seek. “They don’t hear” distinguishes what the speaker experiences from what other characters perceive.
Inspect verbsAsk who acts, who is addressed, and whether the word states, commands, promises, or wishes. Compare “I come” with “Come.”
Inspect nounsTrack “breath,” “soul,” and “name.” Ask how the poems connect bodily life, identity, and death.
Inspect adjectivesThe “last” breath sets a limit. Ethel’s “golden” chair changes what the recurring chair signifies. A small modifier can change the world a noun belongs to.
Inspect adverbs“Now” places the speaker’s knowledge at this moment. In “I wish I could tell you,” knowledge and the ability to communicate it are different things.
Inspect contractions and negationIn “They don’t hear,” don’t means do not: the negative matters to the difference between Ethel and the onlookers. A contraction may also affect voice and rhythm; it is not automatically informal or deficient.
Inspect formNotice line breaks, spacing, repeated openings, and changes to an established pattern. An isolated word can receive emphasis that a word inside a sentence does not.

Why we can propose an answer—and still test it

An interpretation is accountable to evidence. We can compare how well different readings explain the words, their placement, their contexts, and the connections between poems.

  • What supports “come”? It is a verb; it appears near the mystery; it becomes conspicuous in Ethel’s passage; and calling and response recur around death.
  • What remains uncertain? The poems never explicitly say that “come” completes the earlier passage. A connection can be persuasive without being an announced solution.
  • What could challenge it? “Tankas” also contains commands such as “Go,” “Stop,” and “Rest.” A competing answer should explain the death context and the later poems at least as well.

We can also ask what kind of “coming” each passage imagines. The wind’s destructive call and Ethel’s vision of release need not mean the same thing. The connection becomes richer when we preserve those differences.

A claim we can defend: “We propose that the unnamed verb is ‘come’ because Smith connects calling and response with death in ‘Tankas’ and develops that pattern in the two Ethel poems. This is a supported reading, not an explicit answer supplied by the book.”

If we want to take the question further

Research other readings

We could search for the exact phrase “encased in a verb” together with “Patricia Smith,” then examine scholarship, reviews, and interviews. Record who makes a claim, which passage they discuss, and what evidence they give. A search result about Smith’s use of form is not necessarily an answer to this particular question.

Agreement could strengthen or complicate our reasoning. Disagreement could reveal something we missed. Finding no discussion would not prove that nobody has asked—or that our answer is wrong. This page does not claim that this research has already been done.

Ask Smith a specific question

“In ‘Tankas,’ a speaker knows a verb that holds the soul just before death but cannot tell us. We connected ‘I come’ earlier in that poem with the repeated ‘Come’ in ‘Ethel’s Sestina’ and the arrival in the next poem. We think those connections allow us to propose ‘come.’ Did you think of these passages as answering one another, or did that relationship develop differently as you wrote?”

Her response could tell us about her intentions and writing process. We would then consider it alongside the words on the page. If she intended another answer—or wanted the word to remain unnamed—we could reconsider our claim and explain which observations still support it.

Text: Patricia Smith, Blood Dazzler (Coffee House Press, 2008), “Tankas,” pp. 38–39; “Ethel’s Sestina,” pp. 45–46; “Didn’t Need No Music, Neither,” p. 47. This page reconstructs the class’s proposed interpretation, rather than a transcript or a statement of Smith’s confirmed intention.
