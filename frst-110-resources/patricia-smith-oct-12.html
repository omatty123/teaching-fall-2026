Her life, briefly

1955Born in Chicago, West Side. Her parents came north in the Great Migration, her father from Arkansas, her mother from Aliceville, Alabama.
Age 8Decides she will be a writer.
ChicagoFinds poetry at a reading in a Chicago blues club, then the slam at the Green Mill.
1990sFour-time National Poetry Slam champion, the most successful in its history. Journalist at the Chicago Sun-Times and the Boston Globe.
1991First book, Life According to Motown.
1998Resigns from the Globe after admitting she invented people and quotes in her columns (New York Times). Poetry becomes her life’s work.
2008Blood Dazzler, her MFA thesis, a National Book Award finalist.
2012Shoulda Been Jimi Savannah, a memoir in verse about her family.
2017Incendiary Art: Emmett Till, Rodney King. Pulitzer finalist.
2023Unshuttered: voices for 19th-century photographs of Black Americans. Joins Princeton.
2025The Intentions of Thunder, new and selected poems. National Book Award. She turns 70.
Jan. 2025Speaks at Lawrence’s University Convocation in Memorial Chapel, while first-year students read Blood Dazzler.
2026Lawrence’s Creator-in-Residence. Pulitzer finalist again.

The person, in her own words

She has been here before

At Convocation in January 2025 she told Memorial Chapel how a persona poem first hooked her: a man cutting the hair of people about to go to the gas chamber. “It was the most riveting, life-changing thing.” “The best thing I can do as a poet is tell you I am just like you.”
As Creator-in-Residence in 2026: “When you’re a writer, you’re a witness.” Her influences: “my father, who encouraged my voice; Gwendolyn Brooks, who encouraged my vision, and the poetry slam, which taught me the importance of empathy and audience.” On students: “In most cases, they’ve found their voice; they just don’t trust it.”

Lawrence University, Jan. 27, 2025 · Lawrence University, Jan. 16, 2026

Ask next: You met Lawrence first-years last year. What did they ask that you still think about?

Her father’s stories

Her father, Otis Douglas Smith, was the “raconteur of Washington Boulevard, griot of the ghetto.” He worked at a candy company and came home each night with the day’s characters. “Even the trees outside our tenement windows had names.”
School gave her one poem: “Robert Frost did something in the woods … and we were done.”

Smith, “On How a Poet Ages,” Literary Hub, 2025

A second throat

She went to a poetry festival in a Chicago blues club expecting to laugh at the poets. Gwendolyn Brooks was there, listening to high schoolers. Poetry “was really accessible.” Open mics felt “like a second throat. It’s another way to speak.”

Smith, with Joseph Ross, 2014

The slam

“The slam was my fever, my teacher, my critic, my glow, my shadow.” Sunday nights in “a long neon-washed room,” writing a poem just before taking the stage.
She describes her younger self: “She don’t know line break. She don’t know iamb. … She knows stage and slam and people’s faces when she poems.”

Smith, Literary Hub, 2025 · The Intentions of Thunder, quoted in the Chicago Sun-Times, 2025

Her father’s death

Her father was shot and killed. “My father was murdered.” Later poems remember him; one calls her “a daughter who writes screams.”

Smith, Literary Hub, 2025 · Chicago Sun-Times, 2025

The persona poem

For “Skinhead,” she wrote in the voice of a young white supremacist. “The job of a poet is to witness.” Her aim “was not to try to humanize him, but to bring us back to center.” In a persona, “you open your body to somebody else’s story.”

Smith, with Saeed Jones, Poetry Foundation, 2021

Who poems are for

She read a poem in the voice of an undertaker preparing a young man shot dead. At a public housing meeting, a mother who had lost her son got up and left the room. “We write not for audiences but for other human beings.” “Poetry isn’t a recreational activity. It is a calling out, a beckoning near, a circle of arms.”

Smith, Literary Hub, 2025

Starting over

After leaving journalism she earned an MFA, raised a granddaughter, and taught at the College of Staten Island. Poetry is “how I breathe and speak to the world.” Of the past: “People have to give you a chance to be who you are now.”
In 2008: “When I got fired from the Globe, being a poet saved my life because I had a way to process what was happening that had nothing to do with my identity as a reporter.” “Ten years later, I hear no echo.” “I’m totally past it.”

Rachel L. Swarns, New York Times, 2015 · David Medaris, Isthmus, 2008

Learning form

In graduate school “I had really dedicated myself to learning craft.” Forms go in a “toolbox,” and some poems wait because they ask “for something you do not yet know how to do.” “Forms are magic.”

Lauren K. Alleyne, 2018 · Saeed Jones, Poetry Foundation, 2021 · Tupelo Quarterly, 2025

Her own story

Shoulda Been Jimi Savannah is about being “first generation up north.” Her mother would not read her books. “We are in charge of the telling of our own stories. If we don’t do it, someone else will.”

Joseph Ross, 2014

“I am fearless”

“There is nothing I won’t write about, and nothing I won’t say out loud.” “Silence is not the way to live it.” On becoming others: “I can’t be a writer and just be one person. I have to be able to be at least a part of anyone.”

David Medaris, Isthmus, 2008

Teaching

She has taught in public schools and prisons. “Students haven’t found their voices yet; prisoners have had their voices taken away.”

David Medaris, Isthmus, 2008

At seventy

Choosing her new and selected poems meant “clawing back through both your triumphant and dismal decades.” Poets see “through the widened gaze of witnessing.” “We don’t age until we do.”

Smith, Literary Hub, 2025

On Blood Dazzler, and what to ask next

How it began

The St. Bernard Parish nursing home deaths stayed with her: “the room is dark, the water is rising.” “It started out as one poem.” “I did not intend on writing a whole book!”

Lauren K. Alleyne, 2018 · Joseph Ross, 2014 · KNPR, 2016

Ask next: What changed when “34” became part of a whole book?

Why she kept going

After a reading of “34,” a restless woman told her, “They had Mardi Gras, didn’t they?” People “just wanted it to be gone.” “I don’t want to give you a chance to get out of a poem like that.”
“There were those who refused to acknowledge a stark reality—an era, indeed an entire culture, had been sacrificed to the water. That’s when I decided to keep writing.” Black audiences react differently: “There’s more angry acknowledgement. We’re not surprised by anything this country does.”

David Medaris, Isthmus, 2008 · Saeed Jones, Poetry Foundation, 2021

Ask next: Which poem did you write so a reader could not get out of it?

Not from the Gulf

“I’m one voice writing about [Hurricane Katrina]. I am not the story.” It felt “like walking a tightrope the whole time.” A witness “can be corrected by people who were actually there, and that’s fine. I’m looking for that.” And: “there are no stories a poet shouldn’t reach for.”

Saeed Jones, Poetry Foundation, 2021 · Joseph Ross, 2014 · Lauren K. Alleyne, 2018

Ask next: Did someone who was there ever correct you? Did a poem change?

Why the storm speaks

“This line in Blood Dazzler is one of the first lines that came to me: ‘Every woman begins as weather.’” (p. 1) “Katrina’s voice is what eventually gave the book its shape.” “I knew no one would expect to hear the storm to speak, and I needed her to help make sense of the chaos.”
“I didn’t do news research. I tried to find people that nobody saw. Or voices nobody heard.”

Jon Riccio, The Volta, 2016 · Saeed Jones, Poetry Foundation, 2021 · Joseph Ross, 2014

Ask next: How did you decide when we hear Katrina and when we hear someone suffering because of her?

Ethel and the sestina, pp. 45–46

“In writing this persona poem in Ethel’s voice, I wanted her to triumph.” The sestina “mirrored the way elderly women speak, returning again and again to the same idea, the comfortable words.”

Smith, “Poet’s Choice,” Washington Post, 2009

Ask next: The end words return, but “Come” breaks in on p. 46. Why there?

The other Katrina books

Asked in 2008 which books best complement Blood Dazzler, such as Chris Rose’s One Dead in Attic and Tom Piazza’s Why New Orleans Matters: “I haven’t read any of them, at first because I didn’t want to be creatively buffeted about by their content, then because I just can’t find the time.” The book she recommends most to poets is James Lee Burke’s Louisiana novel In the Electric Mist with Confederate Dead.

David Medaris, Isthmus, 2008

Ask next: Have you read the New Orleans writers since? Did any of them change how you see the book?

Counting syllables, counting bodies: “Tankas,” p. 38

“You can’t look directly at death unless you can contain it.” The tanka is “a taming of what refuses to be tamed.” “Working in such a terse, controlled form didn’t change the truth, it just slowed my approach to it. It helped me rein in rampaging emotion. Concentrating on the syllable count gave me a way to confront the body count.”

Jon Riccio, The Volta, 2016

Ask next: Was there a death in the book that no form could contain?

Form for chaos

“Form is a device in my toolbox, accessible to me when it’s needed. Chaos becomes something other when it’s controlled. I love how it’s possible to take a sprawling, unwieldy story and give it lyrical boundaries.”

Jon Riccio, The Volta, 2016

Ask next: Which poem in the book pushed hardest against its boundaries?

If Katrina wrote the blurb

Asked how Hurricane Katrina would blurb the book: “You mean Blood Dazzler? What a weird question. I suppose: ‘Patricia Smith dared to lock eyes with a storm—and thought she saw me.’”

Jon Riccio, The Volta, 2016

Ask next: Katrina says you only “thought” you saw her. What did you miss?

The title

In 2008: “I have no idea what those two words mean.” In 2016: she first typed them as a placeholder at the end of “Siblings,” p. 75. “They didn’t mean anything at the time.” By the end, “those two words had stretched to fit.” “It was darkness and sparkle. It was menace and the memory of a clear, untroubled sky.”

David Medaris, Isthmus, 2008 · Jon Riccio, The Volta, 2016

Ask next: What did those words come to hold that they did not hold at first?

Poets and the news

Students now “don’t know what I’m talking about,” so she tells what happened before she reads. “There are some places where you go to the news for the news, and to the poets for the truth.”

Joseph Ross, 2014 · Saeed Jones, Poetry Foundation, 2021

Ask next: We were born after Katrina. What do you want a reader our age to take from these poems?

Back to her old poems

In The Intentions of Thunder she “enters into dialogue with her earlier poems,” the Pulitzer board wrote. Katrina is there, twenty years on.

Pulitzer Prizes, 2026 · Chicago Sun-Times, 2025

Ask next: Reading Blood Dazzler again for the new book, what would you write differently now?

Why some readers are not enchanted

One word for it is narcissism: she is the center of every story she tells, including other people’s. The evidence is her own words.

1. Grandiosity

She describes herself in absolutes.

“I am fearless. There is nothing I won’t write about, and nothing I won’t say out loud.”

Isthmus, 2008

2. Her experience, made everyone’s law

What is true for her is stated as true for all.

“You can’t look directly at death unless you can contain it.”

The Volta, 2016

“There are no stories a poet shouldn’t reach for. There just aren’t.”

Fight and Fiddle, 2018

3. Contempt when the question isn’t hers

The whole book speaks as the storm. When an interviewer asked her to do the same thing once more, she called it weird.

Asked how Katrina would blurb the book: “You mean Blood Dazzler? What a weird question.”

The Volta, 2016

4. No curiosity about the people who were there

She says she welcomes correction from New Orleans, but she didn’t read New Orleans, or the news.

“She can be corrected by people who were actually there, and that’s fine. I’m looking for that.”

Poetry Foundation, 2021

Asked which Katrina books complement hers: “I haven’t read any of them, at first because I didn’t want to be creatively buffeted about by their content, then because I just can’t find the time.”

Isthmus, 2008

“When I was doing the Katrina thing, I didn’t do news research.”

Poetry Foundation, 2021

5. Rules for others, none for herself

Other writers must justify writing outside their own lives. She wrote a whole book outside hers and needs no justification.

To a white writer who wrote about his Black cellmate: “I wasn’t in the cell with you.” “It’s not just doing it; it’s being able to tell me why you’re doing it. A lot of people say, ‘Well, I’m a writer, and I have a right to...’ And that’s exactly the wrong way.”

Poetry Foundation, 2021

On herself and Katrina: “I’m not from the Gulf region, and I don’t have relatives from the Gulf region.”

Poetry Foundation, 2021

“There are no stories a poet shouldn’t reach for. There just aren’t.”

Fight and Fiddle, 2018

6. Her wrongdoing, retold as her own journey

She resigned from the Boston Globe in 1998 after admitting she had invented characters and quotes. Every later sentence about it is about her: her rescue, her peace, her lessons. The people she misled never appear.

“When I got fired from the Globe, being a poet saved my life.” “Ten years later, I hear no echo.” “I’m totally past it. Learned a lot about myself and my art. So grateful for the lessons.” On her credibility: “it’s always someone else who makes that decision.”

Isthmus, 2008

“People have to give you a chance to be who you are now.”

New York Times, 2015

7. An audience to scold, and to resent

She tells the audience it may not look away, and resents the audience that thanks her for it.

“I don’t want to give you a chance to get out of a poem like that.”

Poetry Foundation, 2021

“I think a lot of white folks, in particular, come to my work looking to hear a Black person say that it’s OK not to pay attention.” Of those who thank her “for giving us the urban viewpoint”: “I feel kind of like their Black pet, their Black friend.”

Poetry Foundation, 2021

From our reading

  • The unnamed verb: In “Tankas,” p. 38, did you have a particular verb in mind? We connected it with “Come” in “Ethel’s Sestina,” p. 46. How we got there
  • Voices beyond death: Ethel says “They don’t hear Come.” We read her as hearing what the living cannot. How did you decide what her voice could tell us?
  • Prayer: “34” includes prayer, and Ethel hears a summons. Do you see a link between prayer and giving the dead a voice?
