Equiano · Chapters 10–12

The Interesting Narrative · Volume II, Chapters X–XII · 1774–1789

These chapters run from a kidnapping case in London to a plantation on the Miskito Shore, and end with a petition to the Queen and an argument about profit. Along the way Equiano is converted, buys enslaved workers, is hoisted by his wrists, and loses a government post for reporting what was stolen.

The three chapters at a glance

Chapter 10 · London and Cadiz

What happens. A free Black cook is kidnapped on the Thames and shipped to St. Kitts. Equiano fails to save him, then is converted at sea.

What it shows. English law will not protect a Black man’s liberty. The freedom he secures is religious, inward, and his own.

Chapter 11 · The Miskito Shore

What happens. He teaches a Miskito prince, buys enslaved workers for Irving’s plantation, is hoisted by ropes and threatened with a musket, and demands wages that nine magistrates refuse to enforce.

What it shows. A free man without a white witness has no wages and no remedy. He is also an owner’s agent, not only a victim, and the chapter does not reconcile the two.

Chapter 12 · London and Sierra Leone

What happens. The Bishop of London declines to ordain him. As commissary for the black poor he reports stolen stores and is dismissed. He petitions the Queen.

What it shows. The state can be petitioned, audited, and out-argued. The case has to be made in the government’s own language.

Eight passages

Chapter 10 · London, April 1774

“I whitened my face, that they might not know me, and this had its desired effect.”

John Annis, a free Black cook on a Turkey ship, is seized at Union Stairs by his former master, William Kirkpatrick, and carried off with two boats and six men. Equiano obtains a writ, hires a court officer, and disguises himself to serve it. The disguise reverses the usual direction of racial passing: he paints himself white to make English law work for a Black man. It fails. Annis reaches St. Kitts, is staked to the ground and flogged, and dies.

Chapter X, full text

Chapter 10 · London, 1774

“I was much astonished to see the place filled with people, and no signs of eating and drinking.”

Invited to a “love feast,” he expects a dinner and finds hymns, public testimonies, buns, and water handed round in mugs. The scene is comic, and it is also reportage: a newly religious African describing English religious life with the same eye he turned on the Eboe and the Miskito. Conversion, here, is social before it is doctrinal.

Chapter X, full text

Chapter 10 · Cadiz, 6 October 1774

“I saw clearly with the eye of faith the crucified Saviour bleeding on the cross on mount Calvary: the scriptures became an unsealed book.”

The conversion comes after months of reading, and after a night in which he dreams of the Last Judgment and begs for time to repent. What follows is the sentence that organizes the whole memoir: every providential circumstance since his kidnapping was in view at that hour. After this point the plot of the book is providence, not accident. A few pages later he tells the crew, and becomes “a barbarian” to them.

Chapter X, full text

Chapter 11 · At sea, January 1776

“How comes it that all the white men on board who can read and write, and observe the sun, and know all things, yet swear, lie, and get drunk, only excepting yourself?”

Prince George, the Miskito king’s son, was baptized in London and returns home on Dr. Irving’s sloop. Equiano teaches him his letters in eleven days and prays with him. The sailors mock the boy, and he stops. His question is the chapter’s indictment, and his answer to Equiano’s warning is worse: if those men go to hell, he will go to hell too. The mission fails because the Christians on board are the argument against Christianity.

Chapter XI, full text

Chapter 11 · Jamaica and the Miskito Shore, 1776

“I went with the Doctor on board a Guinea-man, to purchase some slaves to carry with us, and cultivate a plantation; and I chose them all my own countrymen.”

Equiano buys enslaved people for Dr. Irving’s plantation and picks them from his own nation. He manages their labour, says he treated them well, and asks for his discharge in June over Sabbath-breaking and the state of his soul, not over slaveholding. After he leaves, a brutal overseer replaces him; the workers try to escape in a canoe and drown. The memoir records their deaths and does not resolve his part in them.

Chapter XI, full text

Chapter 11 · The Miskito Shore, June 1776

“Thus I hung, without any crime committed, and without judge or jury; merely because I was a free man, and could not by the law get any redress from a white person in those parts of the world.”

The owner of a sloop, Hughes, wants him to sail to Carthagena, where he says he will sell him. When Equiano refuses, Hughes has him hoisted by wrists and ankles, threatens him with a loaded musket, and jeers that he will not leave the vessel unless he has “St. Paul’s or St. Peter’s faith, and walk upon the water to the shore.” He escapes in a canoe. In Kingston the same rule appears in civil form: nine magistrates refuse to hear his oath against a white captain, and £8 5s in wages is lost.

Chapter XI, full text

Chapter 12 · London, 1787

“It appeared they had never been bought, though paid for by government.”

As commissary for the black poor bound for Sierra Leone, Equiano finds that stores for 750 people were paid for and never purchased, while only 426 could be mustered. He reports it. The agent has him dismissed on 24 March 1787. The Treasury later pays him £50, and he notes that this is “more than a free negro would have had in the western colonies.” The expedition sails into the rainy season short of everything, and many of the settlers did not long survive.

Chapter XII, full text

Chapter 12 · 21 March 1788

“I supplicate your Majesty’s compassion for millions of my African countrymen, who groan under the lash of tyranny in the West Indies.”

He presents the petition in person and signs it “The Oppressed Ethiopean.” It goes over Parliament’s head to the Queen, and it asks her to intercede with the King. The book then turns to political economy: abolition will open African markets to British manufactures, and the present trade supplies “neck-yokes, collars, chains, hand-cuffs, leg-bolts, drags, thumb-screws, iron muzzles, and coffins.” The last page asks what makes an event important unless it makes the reader wiser.

After the book, 1789–1797

The book stops in 1789, with the petition to the Queen. Equiano lived eight more years.
