FRST 110 · Synthesis revision

Three writing upgrades

If you change only three things in your Synthesis revision, change these. Every example comes from your Synthesis papers, with the names taken off.

What's your point? So what? Who cares?

1One thesis, up front, with its "so what"

Many of you found your real argument at the end of the draft and left it there. Others had three or four thesis-like sentences scattered through the paper, each pulling a different way. Your reader needs one argument, stated first, carried through every paragraph, and finished with why it matters.

Start your revision with your conclusion. Move it to the top and make every paragraph prove it.

NOT YETSide by side, they show that disasters can have complicated feelings around who's at fault.

NOT YETIn a time of continued destruction and discrimination, it is important to understand the gravity of both outcomes and push for proper action.

Everyone agrees with both. Saying something "is important" isn't an argument.

BURIEDThe impact of the Great Lakes invasive species and the Hurricane Katrina aftermath could have been lessened had there been proper planning and action.

This was the last paragraph of its paper. It belonged in the first.

CLICHÉTherefore, any action we take must be well-informed and cautious of potential ramifications.

Don't end by retreating to something nice and safe. Say what your argument means for us now.

Let the two works differ. Your thesis comes from the difference, so don't bend one work until it matches the other. Genesis says "all flesh had corrupted its ways upon the earth" (6:12): everyone, not some.

2Say it. Don't announce it.

Throat-clearing is a sentence that gets ready to say something instead of saying it, like a speaker who says "um, so, basically" before the point. It fills space and stalls your reader. Cut it and start with the point.

Hedging is softening a claim so no one can disagree: "may have been," "suggests that," "some of the blame." If your evidence is there, go full out.

BEFOREThis quote demonstrates the effort and persistence that was expressed by Noah to keep his people safe but also their unwillingness to listen.

AFTERNoah tried, but nobody listened.

BEFOREWhile both storms are tragic, without a doubt, and the basis of both destructive elements is the water, there is still a key similarity.

AFTERBoth stories run on faith.

BEFOREFaith in higher power … has a massive impact on the story.

Don't tell us there's an impact. Say what the impact is.

HEDGEDEven worse may have been the human response to these invasions.

AFTEREven worse was the human response to these invasions.

Verbs that announce: shows, demonstrates, discusses, explores, showcases, has an impact on. Verbs that act: blames, abandons, drowns, rewrites, ignores, punishes.

Don't hide the actor. "Stuck," "abandoned," "they": someone did this. Put them in the sentence. When Smith writes "Leave them" (53), someone decided to leave them.

Transitions too: "On the other hand" and "In both works" announce. A good transition says how the next point builds on the last one.

3Quote exactly. Then lean on one word.

Many of you changed a word inside a quotation. And often the best word in the quote went unread. Copy from our text, cite it, then write a sentence about the one word that does the most work.

PAPERAnything I need to do or tweak? (Smith 28)

SMITHAnything specific I need to do / or tweak? (28)

"Specific" makes Brown worse. That one word is evidence.

PAPERwalked faithfully with God (Genesis 6:8-9)

GENESISNoah was a righteous man, blameless in his generation; Noah walked with God. (6:9)

For a paper about blame, "blameless" is the word to lean on.

Name who is speaking. Smith writes in many voices. The storm, the Superdome and her imagined Michael Brown are not Smith, and one voice isn't all of them.

Don't overstate. "The Human Condition," "tenfold," "destroyed the city": big claims need evidence, and they need limits. You don't have to make them.
