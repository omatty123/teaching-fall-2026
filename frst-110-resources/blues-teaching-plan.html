The claim across three days

Delta blues is not a sound from one isolated place. It is a practice shaped by an engineered river, by forced and underpaid labor, by recording, and by movement up the Mississippi to Chicago. So the days run flood, then displacement and labor, then migration.

Wednesday, October 14 · Flood as lived history

Patton, “High Water Everywhere,” Parts 1 and 2 · McCoy and Memphis Minnie, “When the Levee Breaks”

  • For: taking apart “natural disaster.” Start from the National Park Service: people’s attempts to control the river’s power and path made the conflict.
  • Patton names town after town: Sumner, Leland, Greenville, Rosedale, Vicksburg. You can follow the flood on a map. He keeps hitting shut doors: “I would go to the hilly country, but they got me barred.” Who gets to move, who is forced to move, who is made to stay?
  • McCoy and Minnie sing the flood before it comes: waiting on the levee, and “I worked on the levee, mama, both night and day.”
  • Form: Patton is where A A B starts to break, so What are the blues? belongs on this day.

Friday, October 16 · Displacement, levee labor, form

Bessie Smith, “Back-Water Blues” · Alice Pearson, “Greenville Levee Blues” · Lonnie Johnson, “Broken Levee Blues”

  • Smith carries the form lesson. Her verses are clean A A B, which is why she drives the 12-bar animation. She recorded it on February 17, 1927, before the big Mississippi flooding that spring, so it is not an eyewitness account of 1927.
  • Pearson is the flood from inside town: the levee breaks, people scream, boats pick people up, “livin’ on the levee, sleepin’ on the ground.”
  • Johnson carries the coercion: made to work the levee, run by the police, jailed, “work, fight, or go to jail.” With the Greenville history on the page (Black families held on the levee, made to work without pay), it runs straight back to the Superdome and the Convention Center in Blood Dazzler.

Monday, October 19 · Recording and migration

Muddy Waters, “Country Blues” (Stovall, 1941) · “I Can’t Be Satisfied” (Chicago, 1948)

  • For: one artist in two recording situations. A Library of Congress and Fisk field recording made on a plantation, then a commercial record made in Chicago. It ties back to Egan’s Chicago and hands off to Parker on the 21st.
  • Form across the week: Smith is strict A A B, Patton bends it, “Country Blues” sings a couplet twice, “I Can’t Be Satisfied” has a verse and a chorus.

Greenville: Princella Wilkerson Nowell, “The Flood of 1927 and Its Impact in Greenville, Mississippi,” Mississippi History Now. River and migration: National Park Service, Stories of the Delta. Field recordings: Library of Congress, Coahoma County, Mississippi, Field Trips, 1941–1942: A Guide.
