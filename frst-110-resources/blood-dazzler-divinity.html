How do we understand divinity?
And why?

Patricia Smith · Blood Dazzler, section three, pp. 50–77

Divinity: a god, gods, or the qualities understood as divine.

In these poems, language about God expresses hopes for protection, a need for someone to hear, explanations of power, and anger at abandonment. Each portrayal belongs to a particular speaker and situation.

Speaking to, about, and as

  • To: “Our father” and “Lord” address someone expected to hear. Father names kinship; Lord names authority. Our makes a collective relationship.
  • About: He and Him refer to divinity. Capitalization marks divine status or reverence; masculine pronouns also give the portrayal a gender.
  • As: Smith creates voices that claim godlike power. Those claims belong to the speakers; they need not be the poet’s beliefs.

In “Their Savior Was Me,” the residents address God, while Katrina listens and speaks about Him. The intended listener and the responding voice are different.

Good, bad, neutral—or unstable

These are readings of the portrayals, not verdicts about whether God exists. Power and goodness do not consistently coincide.

Prayer and the ACA cycle

ACTING / AWARE-ING / CARING

Prayer is what is left when the ACA loop fails.

A proposed connection for our course.

In “34,” action, awareness, and care fail to bring rescue. Yet prayer remains an action: people voice need, sustain attention, and appeal for care. The failure may belong to those who should respond.

Prayer can be both what remains after the cycle fails and an attempt to begin it again. Spoken or silent, it expresses a relationship that suffering puts under pressure.

Laozi: Heaven and Earth

天地不仁，以萬物為芻狗。

A close translation: “Heaven and Earth are not benevolent; they treat the myriad things as straw dogs.” — Daodejing, chapter 5

Straw dogs: ritual figures made of straw. The Zhuangzi describes them being honored before a ceremony, then discarded afterward. Scholars debate how fully this explains Laozi’s image.

One reading: natural processes do not operate for human welfare. The chapter also applies the comparison to the sage, complicating a simple reading of it as cruelty; impartiality is another interpretation.

Our comparison: a storm’s indifference and human abandonment are different. People can recognize vulnerability and respond with care. This is a connection between texts, not an assertion that Laozi and Smith share one theology.
