Full Disclosure: Manipulating Donors

Jenny studies public relations and has recently begun an internship in the fundraising department of Casa Tia Maria. This U.S. nonprofit provides Central American immigrants with temporary shelter, food, clothing, and English classes while they seek housing and work.

Two weeks into the placement, Jenny accompanies Mary, the development director, to a fundraising dinner. Near the end of the evening, a wealthy philanthropist named Robert approaches them. As Mary describes the organization’s needs, her answers about its clients remain vague.

Robert indicates that he wants to support U.S. citizens. Jenny realizes that he misunderstands whom the shelter serves. Mary does not correct him. Robert writes a substantial check, repeats his belief about the people he is helping, and leaves. Jenny has witnessed the exchange while accompanying the director responsible for fundraising.

Source: Ethics Unwrapped, University of Texas at Austin. Based on a university student’s experience, with names and situations changed.

Scholarship Application

Chris is spending the summer abroad with a nongovernmental organization (NGO) that supports ethnic-minority communities. One of its activities helps students apply for university scholarships. Chris works alongside two other student interns.

A supervisor asks Chris to finish an application due the next day. One applicant has withdrawn. The remaining applicant’s draft has little substantive content and many incomplete sentences. She has already left for the day. The supervisor tells Chris to copy the withdrawn applicant’s work and submit it under her name.

Chris expresses concern. The supervisor says the practice is locally accepted and wants the task completed. Chris consults the other interns. They consider finding the applicant and helping her rewrite the application, but time is short. Editing her existing draft would improve its English without supplying the missing content. The supervisor becomes displeased with both the delay and Chris’s consultation with the others.

Source: Ming Jiu Li, Scholarship Application, Kenan Institute for Ethics, Duke University. Adapted from student experience in the Duke case series.

A GIS internship reconsidered

In her early twenties, Mairéad de Róiste held a geographic information systems (GIS) internship at an Irish city council. Her work involved maps and aerial images available internally to council staff and elected councillors.

Her manager instructed her to remove the internal layouts of prisons from those images. The manager said newly elected councillors were associated with a political party connected to prisoners in those locations, and that access to the layouts could lead to misuse.

De Róiste completed the editing and enjoyed learning the software and the council’s data-storage system. At the time, she did not question the request.

Looking back, she describes her wish to perform well, the difference in authority between herself and her manager, and her limited confidence in raising concerns. She also reconsidered her own participation in the decision. Her account does not establish whether the manager’s security concern was justified.

Source: Mairéad de Róiste, Ethical Dilemmas in Early Career: Reflections on a GIS Internship Experience and its Echo in Geospatial Teaching, Cartographic Perspectives 105 (2025), 18–21. First-person retrospective account.
